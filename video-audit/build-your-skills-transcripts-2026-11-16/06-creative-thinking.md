# 6. Creative Thinking

`course-assets/creative-thinking/creative-thinking.mp4` · 3:08 · 510 words

**[0:00]** Think back to the last time someone told you, good idea. It feels great to hear, but why did they actually say it?

**[0:07]** Most likely you saw something they missed, or you found a more effective way to move forward. It's the ability to see a problem differently, question the obvious approach, and find an angle other people missed.

**[0:20]** We often assume this kind of thinking belongs strictly to artists, writers, and musicians. But problem solving requires the exact same skill. Take Steve Jobs. He wasn't a painter or a novelist. But he was highly effective at finding new angles. He looked at early, complicated desktop computers and saw a possibility others overlooked. They could be simplified, made personal, and easy to use. True creativity is noticing the gaps and possibilities that everyone else walks right past, regardless of your profession.

**[0:52]** Creativity isn't a job title or an innate personality trait. It is a habit of thinking that kicks in whenever a standard answer simply isn't enough. This graphic breaks down how that habit looks across four distinct fields. Look at the top left. A lawyer examines the exact same case file, laws, and facts as everyone else, but finds a completely new strategy.

**[1:14]** Next to that, an entrepreneur surveys the market, spots a consumer need that everyone else overlooked, and builds a new way to meet it. Down on the bottom left, when standard blueprints hit a dead end, an engineer designs a solution that sidesteps the roadblock. And finally, a doctor can look at a common set of symptoms and deduce a diagnosis that others completely missed. Across all these fields, the difference between an average result and an exceptional one comes down to this habit of thinking. Today, we have artificial intelligence. You can type a prompt and get a polished standard answer in seconds. But getting an answer is only the beginning. Your advantage is noticing what's missing, connecting ideas from different places, and choosing a better direction. That human advantage isn't a magic gift.

**[2:01]** You can actively build and improve it through specific daily practice. This guide outlines four concrete habits you can use to generate better solutions. The first habit, shown on the top left, is to generate before you judge. Write down several ideas, including the bad ones, before you decide what works. The most obvious, standard ideas usually arrive first. Next, on the top right, ask what if. Take one core rule or assumption about your problem and change it. See what happens if the exact opposite were true. Third, on the bottom left, connect unrelated things. Look for a pattern, feature, or approach from a completely different industry and try applying it to your current roadblock. Finally, step away, then return.

**[2:47]** Focus intensely on the problem and then go take a walk or switch tasks. New mental connections often form in the background when your attention moves elsewhere. These four habits widen your options. Then judgment picks the one that fits. AI widens the options. You still pick the better angle.
