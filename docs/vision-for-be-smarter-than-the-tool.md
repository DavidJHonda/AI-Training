# Vision for Be Smarter Than the Tool

## What this document is

This is a living brainstorming document about what we might do with **Be Smarter Than the Tool** after the course itself is ready.

It is not a finished business plan, launch plan, or statement of commitments. It is a place to collect possibilities, sharpen the vision, record decisions, and keep promising ideas from getting lost.

Use these labels as the document develops:

- **Idea:** worth capturing, but not evaluated yet
- **Explore:** worth researching or testing
- **Decided:** agreed direction
- **Next:** concrete action to take
- **Parked:** intentionally set aside for later

## The starting vision

**Be Smarter Than the Tool** should help young people become capable, informed owners of their relationship with AI.

The goal is not merely to teach students how to operate today's AI apps. Those apps will change. The more durable goal is to help students understand what AI is, recognize what it does well, notice where it can mislead them, and make better decisions about when and how to use it.

Students should leave the course more prepared to:

- use AI effectively without surrendering their judgment
- understand the basic machinery behind AI's behavior
- question confident or persuasive answers
- recognize important risks and limitations
- adapt as the technology changes
- strengthen the human knowledge and skills that make AI more useful
- take responsibility for the final decision and outcome

The course's central promise is contained in its name: **the person using the tool should remain smarter than the tool.**

## What exists today

The current course is an instructor-led AI-literacy web app designed for high-school students, with a self-paced path available through the same material.

Its learning journey moves through seven stages:

1. Start Smarter
2. Work With AI
3. Understand AI
4. Avoid Traps
5. Embrace the Future
6. Build Your Skills
7. Finish Smarter

The course combines written lessons, video versions, interactive activities, labs, and a final assessment. Its current audience is centered on 16-year-old students, beginning with Luke, Nate, and their peers.

## What makes the course distinctive?

These are working hypotheses to test, not final marketing claims.

- It teaches understanding, not just tool operation or prompt tricks.
- It treats practical use, technical foundations, risks, future change, and human skills as one connected subject.
- It is designed for students without talking down to them.
- It acknowledges both AI's potential and its dangers without building the course around hype or fear.
- It uses activities and concrete examples to make difficult ideas accessible.
- Its message can survive changes in particular apps and models: tools change, but informed human judgment remains essential.

## Questions we need to answer

### Purpose and impact

- What is the single most important change we want to see in a student?
- What should a graduate understand, believe, or do differently?
- Is the primary goal literacy, practical capability, safety, career preparation, or a deliberate combination?
- How will we know the course has worked?

### Audience

- Who should experience the first version after Luke and Nate?
- Is age 16 the center of the audience, or simply the starting point?
- What prior knowledge should the course assume?
- Who makes the decision to use it: students, parents, teachers, schools, or another group?

### Experience

- Is the best version instructor-led, self-paced, or hybrid?
- How long should the complete experience take?
- Which lessons are essential, and which could form optional paths?
- What kind of discussion or human facilitation does the course need?

### Distribution and sustainability

- Should the course be freely available, paid, sponsored, licensed, or offered through a mix of models?
- Do we want to operate the course ourselves or equip other people to teach it?
- What support would teachers or facilitators need?
- What maintenance commitment is required to keep the material current?
- What would responsible growth look like?

### Evidence and trust

- What should we learn from the first student pilots?
- Which outcomes can we measure honestly?
- What outside review would strengthen the course?
- How should we communicate what is current, what is uncertain, and when a lesson was last reviewed?

## Experiments to consider

- **Explore:** Have a small group of students complete the course and record where they become engaged, confused, skeptical, or tired.
- **Explore:** Interview students immediately after the course and again several weeks later to see what they retained and changed.
- **Explore:** Ask one or two teachers to review the course for classroom fit and facilitation needs.
- **Explore:** Test a short workshop assembled from a few representative lessons.
- **Explore:** Compare an instructor-led session with a self-paced session.
- **Explore:** Define a small set of before-and-after measures for knowledge, judgment, and AI-use habits.
- **Explore:** Identify which parts of the course are durable and which require a regular update cycle.
