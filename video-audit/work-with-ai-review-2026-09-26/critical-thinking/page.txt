What you know matters. So does what you do with it. Critical thinking helps you question what you hear, examine the evidence, and decide whether it holds up.

What You Know. How You Think.

Knowledge plus critical thinking leads to better questions and better decisions.

Knowledge

What you know helps you understand the subject and recognize when something doesn’t add up.

Critical Thinking

You question the claim, examine the evidence, and consider other explanations before deciding what to believe or do.

Be Smarter Than the Tool.

It isn’t “don’t trust anything,” either. It’s the habit of asking what would have to be true for a claim to hold up.

CRITICAL THINKING IN ACTION

In 2015, headlines around the world announced a delicious discovery: a new study showed that eating chocolate helps you lose weight. Slim by Chocolate! ran one front page.

Did that headline make you pause? That’s critical thinking getting started. The next step is asking whether the evidence supports it. Picture everyone who read it that morning: the reactions went two directions.

Same Claim. Different Thinking.
The claim

Chocolate helps you lose weight.

Face value

First reaction: Sounds great. I believe it.

Critical thinking

Second reaction: Wait. What’s behind the claim?

Pause when a claim sounds exactly like what you want to believe.

THE TRUE STORY

The study was real, but it was flimsy on purpose:

There were only 15 participants, and the study measured 18 different things about them. With that many measurements in such a small group, a chance result could look like an important discovery.
The “research institute” behind it was just a website.
The author was a journalist who designed the whole thing to prove a point: that a bad study with a great headline would fly around the world before anyone checked. It did.

That’s a big part of critical thinking: the ability to read between the lines, especially when the claim is something you’d love to believe.

There are five habits you can build to sharpen your critical thinking. Each one is a question you ask.

Five Habits of Critical Thinking
Is it actually right?

What evidence supports the claim? Does it support the conclusion?

Do I know enough to judge?

Recognize where your knowledge ends. Find out what you need to understand.

What’s missing?

Look for missing information and other explanations.

Why am I convinced?

Is it the evidence, the confident wording, or what you want to believe?

What’s my call?

Decide what to believe or do. You can change your mind when you learn more.

Each habit is a question you ask.

The five questions work on anything you read or hear. Use them with AI, too. AI can help you find a flaw in your thinking, or confidently repeat it. You need to judge which is happening.

AI amplifies whatever you bring to it.

Good thinking in, sharper output.

What’s the problem with these claims?
Read the claim and its evidence.
Choose the problem that weakens the claim.
01
Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both spike in summer, so ice cream must cause drowning.
There’s a hidden factor causing both
✓
CORRECT
It's a hidden third factor.
Hot weather drives both: more people buy ice cream AND more people go swimming. Just because two things happen together doesn’t mean one causes the other. Always ask: is there a third factor driving both?
The sample size is too small to draw conclusions
✕
NOT QUITE
Sample size isn't the issue.
Sample size isn’t the issue here. The data is real and the correlation is real. The issue is what’s causing the correlation. Hot weather is the hidden third factor driving both numbers up.
The data was probably fabricated
✕
NOT QUITE
The numbers are real.
The numbers themselves are real. Both ice cream sales and drowning deaths actually do spike in summer. The trap isn’t fake data, it’s assuming the connection between two real numbers means one causes the other.
02
A commercial on television claims: "9 out of 10 dentists recommend this toothpaste."
9 out of 10 sounds high but it’s actually a small majority
✕
NOT QUITE
Nine out of ten is a strong majority.
The issue isn’t the arithmetic. It’s what the dentists were asked and how the survey was conducted. A high percentage doesn’t answer those questions.
We don’t know how the survey was done or what ‘recommend’ means.
✓
CORRECT
What does ‘recommend’ mean?
Did the dentists choose this toothpaste over other brands, or could they recommend several? How many dentists were asked, and how were they chosen? Those details help you judge what the number actually tells you.
Dentists aren’t qualified to recommend toothpaste
✕
NOT QUITE
Dentists are reasonable people to ask.
Their expertise matters. You still need to know what they were recommending and how the survey was conducted before deciding how much weight to give the claim.
03
Your friend is doing poorly in math. He texts you: "Einstein failed math as a kid, too. I saw it on TikTok."
A famous story like this is widely known, so it’s probably true.
✕
NOT QUITE
Famous isn't the same as true.
A story can spread because it’s memorable or reassuring. Repetition doesn’t establish that it happened. Look for evidence you can trace and check.
Check the evidence behind the claim, not just where you saw it.
✓
CORRECT
Follow the evidence.
‘I saw it on TikTok’ tells you where you heard the story. It doesn’t establish that it’s true. Look for the evidence the creator provides and check whether it supports the claim.
TikTok is unreliable, so the claim is probably wrong.
✕
NOT QUITE
Don't just dismiss the platform.
True and false claims appear on the same platform. Judge the evidence behind this claim rather than accepting or rejecting it because it appeared on TikTok.
04
Headline: "Smartphones are destroying teen sleep!" The study found that teens who use phones in bed report higher rates of insomnia and anxiety.
The study shows a connection, but doesn’t establish what causes what.
✓
CORRECT
A connection isn’t the whole explanation.
Phone use could affect sleep. Teens who can’t sleep could also reach for their phones. Both could happen, or something else could affect both. The finding described here doesn’t settle which explanation is right.
The headline is right. Screen time keeps you awake, that’s just biology.
✕
NOT QUITE
Does this study show that?
A believable explanation still needs evidence. This finding shows a connection between phone use and sleep problems. It doesn’t establish that phones caused those problems.
Phones probably don’t really affect sleep, so the study is wrong.
✕
NOT QUITE
Don’t dismiss the finding.
Yes. Questioning the headline doesn’t mean the study is wrong. The connection is worth investigating. You need more evidence before deciding what causes what.
05
A basketball player wears neon socks for one game and scores a career-high 30 points. He decides: "These socks are magic."
The socks boosted his confidence, and that caused his career high.
✕
NOT QUITE
That explanation still needs evidence.
The socks might have boosted his confidence, and confidence might affect performance. But one game doesn’t establish that this chain caused his career high. Other explanations could fit the same result.
One game isn’t enough to tell whether the socks made a difference.
✓
CORRECT
One game doesn’t establish the cause.
Maybe the socks boosted his confidence. Maybe he slept well or faced a weaker defender. One great game doesn’t tell you which explanation holds up.
Socks are just clothing, so they couldn’t have affected his performance.
✕
NOT QUITE
Don’t rule it out without evidence.
Clothing can affect comfort or confidence, which could affect performance. This game doesn’t establish that the socks helped, but it also doesn’t establish that clothing could never matter.
