Proposed illustration

A photographic over-the-shoulder view of a person comparing two short answers to the same basketball question. A clearly labeled training-feedback panel makes the choice visible. Keep the readable answer fragments beside the photograph, rather than small on a distant laptop.

  1. Compare: two short basketball answers, A and B.
  2. Choose: highlight the answer with an actionable first step.

Learning benefit: Gives “feedback” a concrete meaning: a person compares answers and chooses the more useful one. It prepares learners for the preference-tuning board that follows.

Keep it only if: A learner can identify the comparison and selected answer before the board returns, without confusing training feedback with a normal chat.

Narration and proposed treatment
Next, the model learns from feedback about which answers people find more helpful.

About seven seconds. Use two states with one clear selection. Suggested answer fragments: A “Use good technique.” B “Start close to the hoop.” Label the demonstration “Training feedback.” Show a human preference, not an instant model improvement or an ordinary chat updating weights. Render all text separately for legibility. Narration and total duration stay unchanged.

Course boards: Keep the complete instruction-tuning and preference-tuning boards, their text, highlights, and camera moves. End the insert before the preference-tuning board appears.

Proposed illustration

Two believable students compare Coke and coffee at a table, with the drinks clearly identified and a rating sheet in view. Pair the photograph with a clean six-position strip labeled Sweet, Bitter, Fizz, Heat, Caffeine, and Dark. Show a hand recording one illustrative rating, then guide attention along the six traits as they are named.

  1. Establish: the two drinks and a student rating sheet.
  2. Record: one illustrative rating, such as Coke → Sweet → 9.
  3. Connect: six consistently ordered traits and the 0–10 scale.

Learning benefit: Makes the bridge from a real characteristic to a number tangible. An ordered set of ratings then leads naturally into the existing numerical-profile board and mystery-drink quiz.

Keep it only if: A learner can explain how a characteristic receives a score and how each position keeps the same meaning. The photograph must not crowd the labels or make the analogy look like the actual AI training process.

Narration and proposed treatment
Imagine we’re running a simple taste test. We rate Coke and coffee across six characteristics: Sweet, Bitter, Fizz, Heat, Caffeine, and Dark, using a scale from zero for low to ten for high.

About fifteen seconds. Keep one stable photographic setting and add restrained, narration-timed labels. Ratings are the lesson’s illustrative choices, not laboratory measurements. Do not imply caffeine is measured by tasting. Keep all six labels in the lesson’s order and make the 0-low/10-high scale readable. Do not reveal the later mystery-drink answer early. Preserve the later explanation that AI learns embedding values during training.

Course boards: Replace only the supporting taste-test graphic. Preserve the existing Coke/coffee table, hidden-label quiz and reveal, Coke/Pepsi comparison, seventh-dimension demonstration, taste-versus-AI comparison, and embedding-table lookup.

Proposed illustration

A photographic tabletop demonstration with two coins already at rest under covers, labeled FIRST and SECOND. A hand lifts only the first cover to reveal heads. The second coin stays completely covered. Use a clear “First: heads” label while leaving “Second: unknown” visible.

  1. Before the clue: both results are covered.
  2. New evidence: first coin is heads; second stays hidden.

Learning benefit: Shows exactly what the new evidence tells us—and what it does not. That makes the following reduction from four possible outcomes to two easier to understand.

Keep it only if: A learner sees that only the first result becomes known. The photograph must make uncertainty clearer than the current drawing without rushing the next diagram.

Narration and proposed treatment
We run the two-coin toss again, but this time someone peeks. They tell you the first coin landed on heads.

About six seconds. Use one fixed camera and two matching states, with a simple reveal on the spoken clue. Do not toss again, move or flip either coin during the reveal, show the second result, or calculate the answer on this shot. Composite precise labels separately. Confirm the short window is comfortable in the encoded excerpt.

Course boards: Keep all four-outcome diagrams, elimination animation, 25%→50% calculations, and the explanation that information changed, not the coins. Preserve the opening company imagery and all previously retained material outside this small supporting shot.
