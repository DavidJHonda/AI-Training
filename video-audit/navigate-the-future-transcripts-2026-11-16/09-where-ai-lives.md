# 9. Data Centers

**[0:00]** You type a question, hit send, and an answer starts appearing.

**[0:05]** Behind that chat, computers are doing an enormous amount of math. Imagine a model using one trillion weights at roughly two calculations per weight. On the left, producing one token, such as spot, requires one pass through our example model's trillion weights. That yields our two trillion calculations. Moving to the middle panel, a short answer is about 100 tokens written by the AI. One hundred tokens at two trillion calculations each is about two hundred trillion calculations. On the right, a longer conversation takes about one thousand tokens. One thousand tokens at two trillion each pushes the math into the realm of two quadrillion calculations. These counts cover the tokens the AI writes. They are estimates for an imagined model, not measurements of a real one.

**[0:53]** The ability to run that math is called compute. Now imagine serving millions of people simultaneously. That volume of math is exactly why AI companies need data centers and why they are building more of them. You can think of a data center as a big warehouse packed with thousands of specialized chips called GPUs running around the clock. Here is the inside of an AI data center. Rows of server racks stretch all the way to the far wall glowing with blue status lights. You can see one person walking the main aisle completely dwarfed by the racks.

**[1:28]** Some large AI data centers cover the ground of several football fields and can use as much electricity as a small city. The scale varies by facility, but the mechanical process is consistent. When you hit send and chat GPT, a data center answers.

**[1:43]** Somebody pays for all that arithmetic. Running those computers affects the company using them, but it also directly affects the communities around them. This graphic breaks down what a data center means for its neighbors across four specific effects.

**[1:57]** First is electricity.

**[1:59]** U .S. data centers used about 4 .4 percent of electricity in 2023. Berkeley Lab projected that number could reach 6 .7 to 12 percent by 2028. In some places, this added demand is already raising household bills. Second is water. Chips run hot, some facilities evaporate water to cool them down, and a large data center can use about a million gallons on a hot day. Others do recycle or reuse it. Third is noise. The cooling fans run 24 hours a day. In some towns, neighbors have actually sued over the hum after losing sleep.

**[2:35]** Finally, permanent jobs. While initial construction employs many people, a finished facility may need only 100 to 200 permanent workers. That is roughly the staff of a big supermarket.

**[2:48]** Looking at this panel, we can see the three ways companies are responding to this demand. First, companies are arranging additional electricity supplies for their data centers. Second, they are developing better cooling designs that reuse water, reducing how much new water they need to draw. Third, they are building more efficient chips that can do more work with each unit of electricity. More power meets demand. Better efficiency reduces the resources needed per task. Making tasks more efficient does not automatically mean a smaller total footprint. If the use of AI grows fast enough, the total demand for resources will still rise. One request is a small part of the picture. Running these services simultaneously for millions of people is what makes the infrastructure so large.

**[3:34]** Every big technology has a footprint from streaming video to the cars parked outside. AI's footprint is real, it is growing, and somebody pays it. The company pays in dollars, the grid pays in watts, and the neighborhood pays in water and quiet. None of this is a reason to feel guilty hitting send. It is a reason to be one of the few people who actually understands the machine you are using. Every AI chat costs something real. Now you know what's behind the magic.
