**[0:00]** If you look at YouTube or TikTok, or listen to friends and family, you'll hear a lot of opinions about AI. It can feel like an endless amount of noise. So who do you actually believe? Let's cut through that noise and look at the people who build AI for a living. You might expect them to agree, but they don't. Same field, same evidence, very different bets. First, the optimist, Dario Amadei. He helped build GPT-2 and GPT-3 at OpenAI and then founded Anthropic, the company behind Claude. Amadei says, AI-enabled biology and medicine will allow us to compress the progress that human biologists would have achieved over the next 50 to 100 years into 5 to 10 years.

**[0:45]** But he also admits, humanity is about to be handed almost unimaginable power. And it is deeply unclear whether our social, political, and technological systems possess the maturity to wield it. Next is the warrior, Jeffrey Hinton. He won a Nobel Prize for his ideas that AI runs on. At 75, he left his job at Google so he could warn people about AI. Hinton says, We're actually making new kinds of beings. They have goals. We give them goals, and from those goals, they derive other goals. we don't necessarily know what other goals they'll derive. But even he admits, if we can detect cancer much earlier thanks to AI, fewer people will die from it. None of them has a simple, one-sided view. The optimist sees danger.

**[1:31]** The worrier sees benefits. If the creators themselves are this divided, it becomes clear that even the people who know AI best still don't know where it's actually going. This uncertainty isn't new. Every time a major technology arrives, the smartest people around make highly confident predictions about it. And those predictions miss in both directions. In 1995, astronomer Clifford Stoll predicted that online shopping could not compete with malls. Today, it is a routine part of everyday life. And going back to 1940, Ford Motor Company founder Henry Ford predicted a flying car was coming. We are still waiting for that to become ordinary transportation.

**[2:12]** A technology becomes the future only when people change their habits around it. Machines improve fast, habits change at human speed. Even the experts disagree about where AI is going. So what's a person to do? We think it helps to ask one more question. What's actually in your hands? That's where to put your energy. Let's start on the left side of this framework. This entire column represents external events. You do not dictate how AI will change future jobs. how quickly the underlying technology evolves, or the massive volume of work a machine can suddenly produce. You don't get a say in how other people use AI or what the media decides the next headline predicts. Now look at the right side. This is your actual zone of control.

**[2:58]** First, you can choose to build real depth in a subject you care about. You can choose to stay curious as the landscape shifts. And as you test new things, you can actively decide to keep only the methods and tools that consistently work for you. You can practice coming up with your own original ideas instead of outsourcing your thinking entirely to a machine. You choose exactly how you integrate AI into your workflow and how you collaborate with the people around you. Finally, choose to make something real right now rather than passively waiting to see what happens to the industry. You do not control every outcome, but your choices still matter. Keeping informed about the left column is fine, but you need to put most of your daily energy into the right.

**[3:44]** The cultural conversation around AI is deafening right now, and you cannot control where the technology goes next. But you can build the skill and the judgment to decide what you do next.
