# 6. Does School Matter?

`course-assets/does-school-matter/does-school-matter.mp4` · 3:30 · 576 words

**[0:00]** A lot of people are quietly asking the same question right now. If artificial intelligence can spit out the answer to nearly anything in seconds, do you actually need to sit through years of classes anymore?

**[0:13]** Let's test that. Fast forward a few years. You've landed a great job, and that AI sits right next to you all day, ready to crank out code, drafts, and plans. Your coworker at the next desk is doing the exact same thing. You both have the same job title and you're using the exact same software.

**[0:31]** Then, your boss hands you both an assignment. Plan a new product launch. You each type similar prompts into your AI. Unsurprisingly, the tool gives you both similar plans. Your starting lines are identical. Let's look at this board to see what happens next with those two similar plans. The first part of the plan covers sales projections. Your coworker looks at the numbers the AI generated and simply accepts them. Using the math skills you learned in school, you work through the costs and find a more profitable pricing option.

**[1:02]** Understanding how those numbers fit together allowed you to spot an inefficiency the tool missed, turning a baseline projection into a tangible advantage over your peer.

**[1:12]** Next is the customer email. Your coworker takes the AI's generated draft and uses it exactly as written. Using the writing skills you developed in English class, you make the message clearer and more persuasive. Finally, we reach market research. Your coworker accepts the AI's findings without bothering to check the original sources. Using the research skills you developed in school, you compare sources and identify a more promising group of customers. The tool may be the same. What you bring to it is yours. You started with similar AI answers. You ended with different work. The gap between your plan and your coworker's plan didn't come from a better prompt. It came from knowing exactly which details to question, where the gaps were, and what needed further investigation.

**[1:59]** Those critical instincts do not manifest out of thin air the moment you get hired. They are built up slowly, over years of practice. Writing papers teaches you to organize complex ideas and make a convincing point. Working through difficult math problems forces you to follow logical structures. Researching historical or scientific subjects teaches you to weigh evidence before forming conclusion. That daily, sometimes frustrating repetition in the classroom is exactly what builds the mental architecture required to solve complex problems and deliver superior work. Let's look at this breakdown to see exactly what that classroom work is helping you construct for your future. The first pillar is knowledge. This is your chance to go deep into a subject you actually care about, eventually becoming the person other people turn to for answers.

**[2:49]** The second pillar is skills. You learn to write, build, and work with other people. You practice critical thinking, learning to question claims, weigh the evidence in front of you, and make an informed decision. School is where you get to build what you bring to the work. Find something that interests you and master it. Whatever new software, platform, or technology arrives next, your underlying knowledge and analytical skills remain permanently yours. that is how you stay smarter than the tools you use. The opportunity to learn is already in front of you. Choose something you want to get better at. Start there.
