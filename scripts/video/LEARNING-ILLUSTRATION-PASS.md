# Learning Illustration Pass

Established workflow, named and consolidated 2026-10-08 after the owner asked to
preserve the approach used across the existing videos. Earlier folders named
`illustration-pilot` are historical records; this is an ongoing production practice.

**Purpose:** make a narrated idea easier to understand, follow, and remember by
showing the example, action, comparison, or change at the moment it is taught.
Engagement comes from helping the learner see the point, not adding decoration.

Read this with [Edit Spec](EDIT-SPEC.md), especially sections 5 and 8–8d.
Edit Spec remains the authority for visual/audio treatment; this playbook makes
the illustration workflow and its approved references easy to reuse.

## The style to preserve

- Ground scenes in recognizable student situations. For new people/settings,
  follow section 8d's realistic, photographic editorial treatment: high-school-age
  learners, natural expressions and poses, believable objects, mature composition,
  ordinary clothing, and lighting/color consistent with approved course imagery.
- Show the specific learning action: checking an original source, adding a file
  to a conversation, comparing alternatives, testing recall, or acting on a plan.
  A generic student at a laptop is not enough. Match the subject to the spoken
  example: college comparison must look like colleges, not property shopping.
- Make instructional text and interface states precise. Generate the scene with
  usable screen/paper space, then typeset important words, values, and highlights
  separately when needed. Keep these readable in the encoded 720p video. Avoid
  decorative slogans, invented evidence, and visuals implying guaranteed accuracy.
- Keep related states in the same scene: same student, clothing, room, camera,
  objects, screen geometry, and lighting. Change only what the explanation needs.
  Reuse/edit the approved image instead of independently generating each state.
- Let the narration drive reveals and emphasis. Highlight each spoken dimension,
  score, step, source passage, or takeaway when it is discussed. Retain information
  the viewer needs to compare. Do not reveal an outcome before its teaching cue.
- Use restrained motion: a gentle push, a purposeful reveal, or a change of
  emphasis. Keep readable material stable; do not move it merely to add activity.
- Preserve useful existing drawings and animations. Explanatory diagrams need
  not be photographic. A specifically approved drawn insert, such as Know the
  App's college comparison, does not reset the default style for new people scenes.
- Keep course boards canonical. Supporting scenes complement them; they do not
  redesign, restate, or replace their teaching. Preserve complete-card highlights,
  narration-timed banner emphasis, readable framing, and the standard close.

## Repeatable process

1. **Audit the current section.** Resolve installed videos from `LESSON_VIDEOS`,
   check current lesson content and recent review records, and examine narration
   with the actual visual sequence. Do not revive an old narration flag without
   checking later rolls, renamed lessons, and completed repairs.
2. **Select worthwhile moments.** Identify where a concrete scene or changing
   state would clarify something currently abstract, repetitive, misleading, or
   missing. Record the narrated words, current shot, exact proposed span, teaching
   benefit, and what will remain. Leaving an effective video alone is a valid result.
3. **Use approved references.** Inspect relevant reference frames/clips below
   before writing prompts. Record which reference guides realism, composition,
   text treatment, and state continuity. Prefer a few strong interventions over
   a quota of illustrations per video. Follow the existing plan-approval workflow;
   reuse authorization already given for the same work.
4. **Build scenes and timed states.** Save final images, complete prompts, source
   provenance, state definitions, and exact frame intervals with the build.
   Keep generated scenes separate from deterministic teaching text/overlays.
   Preserve audio, duration, FPS, and unaffected visuals for a visual-only pass.
   Use a fresh candidate version; rebuild from pristine sources where available.
5. **Review in context.** Provide before/after excerpts with surrounding narration
   and the full candidate. Use visible posters and verify actual video playback.
   Inspect encoded frames, all state changes, entry/exit transitions, text,
   continuity, and whether the picture really explains the spoken point.
   Verify copied audio and frame counts; distinguish technical checks from a
   completed listening pass. Iterate on the owner's feedback.
6. **Ship the approved version.** Follow the README's local installation,
   verification, scoped commit, and separate batch-deployment rules. Retain the
   prompts, assets, timing manifest, review, hashes, and approval record so the
   next pass can reproduce the treatment rather than guess at it.

This process applies both to existing-video improvements and supporting scenes
within a full production build. It does not silently expand a visual-only task
into narration changes or a redesign of the course.
