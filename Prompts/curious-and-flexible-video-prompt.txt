Core teaching: curiosity helps students notice changes that may matter. Flexibility helps them test those changes and adopt only what improves their work. Curiosity does not mean chasing everything new. Flexibility does not mean changing everything.

Open with the school basketball story. A play worked all season, then another team learned how to stop it. The captain needs curiosity to look for a better play and flexibility to stop relying on the old one. Explain that these are different skills: curiosity notices and explores; flexibility tests and adjusts.

Apply the idea to AI. New models, tools, and ways of working appear quickly. Explain both traps: ignoring every change can make you miss something useful; chasing every announcement can turn keeping up into the work. The better approach is to notice changes that might matter, then test them before changing how you work.

Introduce the biology-study example from the Markdown. The student normally gives AI biology notes and asks for practice questions. A friend suggests a different approach. The student should neither switch immediately nor dismiss it. Curiosity means learning enough to decide whether the idea deserves a test.

Then show board 1, “Stay Curious,” full-frame before walking all four complete cards. Give each card enough time to teach both the action and its reason:

1. Use AI regularly. Real use creates a baseline, so students can recognize when results or abilities change.
2. Check what changed occasionally, not constantly. Ask: What changed? Is it available to me? Could it improve something I already do?
3. Follow one reliable source. A useful source explains the change, who can use it, and its limits; one good source is better than a feed full of hype.
4. Compare with others. Ask what improved and what went wrong, not only what is new. Different people notice different strengths and problems.

Say the handoff clearly: curiosity finds a possibility; flexibility decides whether that possibility belongs in your work.

Return to the same biology example for board 2, “Be Flexible.” Hold the full board, then walk all four complete cards. Again, teach the reason under every heading:

1. Start with a real need. “I want to find gaps in what I know before Friday’s biology test” is a need. “I want to use the newest feature” is not.
2. Test it on familiar work. Use the same biology chapter or notes so errors and differences are easier to recognize.
3. Compare the results. Put old and new approaches side by side and judge quality, time, effort, and reliability. Faster is not better if it is less accurate; polished is not better if it misses the learning goal.
4. Keep what works best. Adopt the new approach when the improvement is real. Keep the old method when the new one adds effort or problems. Students may also combine the best parts of both.

Then say: “Curiosity opens the door. Flexibility decides whether to walk through it.” Students do not need to try everything first. They need to notice, test, compare, and adjust.

End on board 3 and speak exactly: “Be curious. Be flexible.” “That is how you keep up when the technology keeps changing.” Hold board 3 through the final frame.
