# 4. Questions Matter

`course-assets/questions-matter/questions-matter.mp4` · 3:42 · 609 words

**[0:00]** AI can help with almost any kind of work, but to actually get useful help from it, you have to know what to ask. For decades, technology has steadily reduced the friction of getting answers, and the less time we spend chasing a fact, the more time we have to spend framing the actual question. In the library era, getting an answer meant traveling to a building, searching a physical catalog, tracking down books, and taking notes by hand. That process easily took half a Saturday. Then came the search era. You ran queries, opened a dozen tabs, judged which sites to trust, and copied the useful pieces. Finding the answer dropped to an hour or two. Now we have AI.

**[0:45]** You open an app, type a prompt, and the answer appears on your screen in seconds. Half a day, then an hour, now seconds. Technology has made getting answers incredibly cheap, But that does not make human effort less valuable. It just changes where our value lives.

**[1:03]** Before AI, the sheer work of locating a good answer was valuable. You had to know where to look and what to trust. With AI, the answer is instant. The hard part is deciding which questions to ask, and judging whether the output actually solves your problem. The modern reality is actually quite simple. Answers got cheap. Questions didn't. Asking good questions is one of humanity's oldest and most tested skills. Socrates taught his students by doing almost nothing but asking questions. He forced them to examine their own assumptions rather than simply handing them conclusions.

**[1:39]** The scientific method works the same way. It starts with a precise question worth testing. As the famous saying goes, if you have an hour to save the world, spend the first 55 minutes finding the right question. So what separates a strong question from a weak one? There are four specific qualities, and none of them actually rely on AI. This chart outlines the first two qualities of a good question, being open-minded and specific. First, open-minded. You haven't picked the answer in advance. A leading question isn't research, it's a request for backup. Compare a weak approach—homework doesn't help students learn. Can you help me prove it ?—to a much better one—what does the research actually say about homework and learning? Second, specific.

**[2:25]** Give enough detail about your situation to get an answer that fits. A weak question asks, how do I get better at sports? A better specific question says, I play basketball as a point guard. Sometimes I lose the ball against pressure. What drills fix that? By providing exact details while keeping your mindset open, you stop the machine from feeding you generic advice or just confirming what you already want to hear. Moving to the graphic, we see the final two qualities, being on target and open-ended. Third is on target. Ask about the problem you actually need to solve. The first question that comes to mind might miss it. A weak target is, what energy drinks work best for staying awake in class? A better target digs deeper. I keep falling asleep in first period, even when the class is interesting.

**[3:12]** What could be making me so tired and what can I change? Finally, open-ended. invite an explanation, not just a yes or no. Leave room for an answer you didn't expect. A weak prompt asks, should I join the debate team? A better open-ended prompt asks, what would joining debate add to my week and what would I have to give up for it? Answers got cheap, questions didn't. Frame the problem, ask the next better question.
