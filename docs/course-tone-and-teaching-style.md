# Be Smarter Than the Tool: Tone, Teaching, and Writing Style

## Purpose and use

Use this guide when writing or revising educational lessons. It captures the voice and teaching approach of **Be Smarter Than the Tool**, an AI course for high school students, and is designed to be added to an existing AI training or teaching skill.

The primary source is the lesson content: openings, explanations, worked examples, comparisons, teaching boards, and closing messages. TRY ITs, LABs, quizzes, and activity mechanics do not define this guide. A lesson should teach its central idea even when the reader skips the activity.

These instructions distill recurring strengths in the course. They are an editorial guide, not a claim that every existing sentence follows every rule.

### Suggested instruction for the existing skill

> Read this guide before drafting or revising lesson content. Write for a capable beginner. Begin with something the learner can picture, build the explanation in connected steps, introduce terminology when it becomes useful, and show what the learner can do with the idea. Keep the voice conversational, specific, and respectful. Preserve accuracy, explain the limits of analogies, and end on the lesson's central insight. Treat exercises as supporting practice. Adapt examples to the actual audience and preserve the author's identity.

## 1. The teaching promise

Help the learner understand enough to make a better judgment.

The course keeps returning to a few connected ideas:

- Understanding how a tool works helps you recognize its strengths and failures.
- Your knowledge changes the questions you ask and the mistakes you can catch.
- Getting an answer is only part of the work. You decide whether it is useful and supported.
- Skills develop through practice, feedback, and taking responsibility.
- You can act now without knowing exactly what the future holds.

These ideas should shape the explanation. Repeating the course slogan is optional. The lesson earns its message by showing the learner why it matters.

For another subject, preserve the same promise: give the learner a usable understanding and a better way to make decisions.

## 2. Who is speaking, and to whom?

Write as a knowledgeable, approachable guide talking to an intelligent beginner. In this course, the reader is roughly 16, with everyday experience and little assumed technical knowledge.

Assume the learner can handle a difficult idea when you explain it well. Explain prerequisites without apologizing for the subject or calling it easy. A confused reader needs a clearer next step.

The original course also has an authentic peer voice: Luke and Nate are students who helped build it. Their school experiences, family details, and hockey references belong to them. When applying this guide elsewhere, use the actual author's perspective. Do not invent first-person experiences or claim to be the course's creators.

### Voice settings

| Quality | What it looks like in the writing |
| --- | --- |
| Conversational | Address the reader as “you.” Use contractions and words someone would say aloud. |
| Direct | State what happens, why it happens, and why the learner should care. |
| Respectful | Take the learner's questions seriously. Let the explanation carry the difficulty. |
| Warm | Recognize familiar frustrations, uncertainty, and effort without excessive reassurance. |
| Lightly funny | Use an occasional recognizable absurdity or specific human detail. |
| Confident and careful | Explain clearly while marking uncertainty and the boundaries of a claim. |
| Encouraging | Point to something the learner can practice, decide, or build. |

Avoid corporate language, forced teen slang, constant enthusiasm, and praise with no evidence. Encouragement should leave the reader with a possible next move.

## 3. Start where the learner already lives

Open with a situation, question, observation, or small problem that makes the idea worth understanding.

Examples from the course:

- **What Is AI?** asks a desk for history-project ideas. Its silence gives the explanation somewhere concrete to begin.
- **Beyond the Average** takes seriously the question of whether school still matters when AI can produce answers.
- **Embeddings** starts with a student ID: a number identifies a student but tells you little about them.
- **Document Trap** uses a basketball rulebook whose tournament exception changes the answer.
- **Curious & Flexible** begins with a basketball play that worked until another team learned to stop it.

These openings create a reason to learn. They are part of the explanation.

Choose details the audience can recognize: an essay draft, an unfamiliar rule, a team, a lunch table, a first assignment at work. When adapting to adults, choose situations from their lives. Keep the teaching move and change the setting.

A direct opening also works. **People Skills** begins with a simple observation about people needing one another. Every lesson does not need a story, joke, or dramatic surprise.

## 4. Build the explanation one useful step at a time

A recurring lesson shape is:

1. **Give the learner something to picture.** Establish the situation or question.
2. **Show what needs explaining.** Reveal a gap, surprising result, or limitation in the obvious approach.
3. **Explain what happens.** Walk through the mechanism or reasoning in an order the learner can follow.
4. **Name the concept.** Introduce the term near the example that gives it meaning.
5. **Show the implication.** Explain what this changes about how the learner understands or handles the situation.
6. **Land the idea.** Finish with a short conclusion the explanation has earned.

Use this as a reasoning sequence. Vary the visible structure to fit the subject.

### Let the problem create the need for the next idea

The **Tokens** lesson considers assigning every word a number. New words, slang, typos, and other text expose the limits of that approach. Reusable pieces then solve a problem the reader already understands.

The **Embeddings** lesson describes drinks with numerical ratings. Two drinks initially match, so another dimension becomes useful. The student can see why the concept exists before being asked to remember its name.

This is stronger than listing terms and definitions at the start. Each new idea should answer a question the previous step raised.

### Keep the important reasoning on the page

Do not jump from a named concept to a conclusion the beginner cannot reconstruct. In **AI is Math**, the coin example shows which possible outcomes remain after a clue. The explanation accounts for the changed probability.

Concise writing still needs the steps that make the result understandable.

## 5. Use examples as part of the reasoning

An example should do a specific job: reveal a distinction, expose an assumption, show a process, or make a consequence visible.

### Stay with a useful example

Return to the same situation as the explanation develops. Familiarity frees the reader to focus on the new idea.

- The dog-name question connects probability, generating an answer, and variation between answers.
- The CAT/IT sentence connects context, attention, and layers.
- The basketball rulebook connects retrieval, a missing exception, and checking the original passage.
- The lunch-table situation in **Support Trap** connects comforting words to what changes in the student's life.

When an example returns, add something. A callback should advance understanding rather than repeat the previous explanation.

### Make comparisons fair and specific

Hold the situation steady so the learner can see what changed. **Questions Matter** shows a weak question and an improved version. **Flattery Trap** compares responses to the same essay opening. **Work Changes** follows the same assignment through different ways of doing it.

Compare corresponding details. Explain why one response, choice, or approach serves the purpose better.

### Explain the reveal

In **Hallucination**, an answer includes an impressive-sounding study. The lesson reveals that the study does not exist, then explains why its details made it persuasive.

A surprising moment starts the teaching. The explanation afterward delivers it. Keep fictional demonstrations clearly distinguishable from real events and evidence.

## 6. Make technical ideas accessible and accurate

### Attach terminology to something visible

Introduce a term with its plain meaning and a concrete referent. In **Embeddings**, the reader first sees an ordered row of drink ratings. The lesson then names the row a vector and its positions dimensions.

Use the technical word after introducing it. Keep names consistent so the learner can follow the same object through the explanation.

### Map analogies explicitly

Explain which part of the familiar example corresponds to the technical idea. Then identify the limit that matters.

- A student ID illustrates identification. It does not describe a person's characteristics.
- Drink ratings show how a row of numbers can describe something. The course then explains that AI learns its values and does not use neatly named taste dimensions.
- The Chinese Room illustrates a question about convincing answers and understanding. The lesson states that an LLM does not operate through a literal rulebook.

Avoid carrying an analogy beyond the relationship it helps explain. Add a brief boundary where a learner could otherwise form the wrong model.

### Label simplifications where they occur

Say when words stand in for tokens, numbers are illustrative, or a model is hypothetical. State conditions that affect the result. Show units and what the numbers represent.

Use the smallest caveat that prevents a meaningful misunderstanding. Keep it close to the claim it qualifies.

### Preserve distinctions

The course's stronger explanations separate ideas that are easy to confuse: identification and description, generated facts and misread sources, missing information and false information, supportive language and practical support.

Ask what false conclusion a beginner might take away. Repair the wording before adding another example.

## 7. Teach judgment with concrete moves

Move from a general value to an observable behavior.

“Think critically” becomes questions about evidence, missing information, and why a claim feels convincing. “Improve your people skills” becomes listening through someone's answer and asking a follow-up question. “Stay flexible” becomes testing a new tool on familiar work and comparing the results.

Short groups of moves help readers remember what to do. Each move needs a clear verb and enough explanation to use it. Let the content determine the number of moves.

Explain when the advice applies. **Evaluate the Results** scales the depth of checking to the task, the learner's knowledge, and the consequences of being wrong. **Art of Prompting** explains that a quick question does not need every prompting technique.

Avoid turning useful habits into rituals the learner must perform every time. Give the reader criteria for choosing the next step.

## 8. Balance warmth, humor, and seriousness

Humor in the course usually comes from something specific: expecting a desk to answer, a student stealing fries, or an impossible solution to a crowded school schedule.

Use humor briefly and move back into the explanation. It should help the learner picture or remember the idea. Never make the learner's confusion the joke. Avoid adding slang merely to signal that the audience is young.

When the subject involves harm or vulnerability, use a calm, humane voice. **Support Trap** acknowledges that relief from a chat can be real while explaining what a person can do that the exchange has not accomplished. Its serious case is presented with a limit on what the account establishes.

For future-facing topics, distinguish evidence, possibilities, and predictions. **Loudest Voices** makes room for experts with mixed views. **Make Your Move** brings uncertainty back to decisions a student can make now.

Match the emotional weight to the subject. Offer agency without promising a particular outcome.

## 9. Write prose that sounds natural aloud

- Use familiar words and active verbs: ask, compare, check, choose, explain, build.
- Give each paragraph one main job. Short paragraphs make the reasoning easier to follow.
- Mix short landing sentences with longer explanatory ones. Save fragments for emphasis.
- Make transitions explain the connection between ideas. The reader should know why the next paragraph follows.
- Use questions when they open a real problem or invite a useful judgment. Avoid a stream of rhetorical questions.
- Use headings for meaningful turns in the explanation. Skip headings that repeat the sentence immediately above them.
- Use bold selectively for a new term or a central point.
- Use numbered lists for sequences and small sets of moves. Keep explanatory prose around them when the reader needs the reasoning.
- Avoid em dashes in new copy. This is an explicit course writing preference.
- Remove inflated phrases such as “unlock your potential,” “revolutionize your learning journey,” and “in today's rapidly evolving landscape.” State the actual benefit or change.

Read the draft aloud. Fix places where the voice becomes formal, breathless, repetitive, or hard to follow. Do not compress the entire lesson into slogans.

## 10. Give teaching visuals a clear job

The course's boards often carry substantial instruction: a process, a comparison, a numerical example, or a short set of moves. Their teaching content belongs in this guide's source material.

When planning a visual, identify exactly what the learner should notice. Introduce enough context to understand it, then explain what the pattern or result means. Avoid repeating every label in the surrounding prose.

For a text-only lesson, preserve that teaching in words, a table, or a worked example. The explanation should not depend on an unavailable image.

Labels such as “Board 1,” image filenames, and production notes are source-document scaffolding. They are not part of the learner-facing voice and need not appear in another teaching skill's output.

## 11. End on the insight

The course commonly closes with one or two compact lines. They capture what the learner now understands or what judgment follows from it.

Examples from the lessons:

> The tool answers. You evaluate.
>
> Read. Understand. Validate.

*Evaluate the Results*

> A missing passage can change the answer.
>
> Ask for the passage. Then check it.

*Document Trap*

> The tool is rented.
>
> The skills are yours to keep.

*Build Your Skills opener*

Write the close after the explanation. It should be specific enough to belong to this lesson and accurate enough to stand on its own.

The course's explicit editorial guidance is to end on the lesson's landing point. Put any necessary orientation at the beginning of the following lesson. Do not weaken the ending with a routine preview, generic congratulations, or an additional idea that needs explaining.

## 12. Examples of applying the style

The passages below are newly written illustrations of the style, not quotations from the course.

### Explain an abstract idea through a situation

**Generic draft:**

> Contextual specificity facilitates more effective AI-assisted ideation.

**In this style:**

> You ask AI for fundraiser ideas. It suggests a concert. Your club has six people, $80, and two weeks. Tell AI those details first. Now it has a better chance of suggesting something you could actually organize.

**Why it works:** The reader can see what information was missing and why supplying it changes the task.

### Turn an instruction into a usable judgment

**Generic draft:**

> Always critically evaluate generated content for accuracy and relevance.

**In this style:**

> AI says the application closes Friday. Open the official application page and check the date. A wrong deadline could cost you the opportunity, so this answer deserves a closer look.

**Why it works:** It names the claim, the check, and the consequence that makes the check worthwhile.

### Make encouragement concrete

**Generic draft:**

> Embrace lifelong learning to thrive in an AI-driven future.

**In this style:**

> Pick something you want to get better at. Use AI to help you start, then compare the result with what good work looks like. Improve one part. That gives you something to build on the next time.

**Why it works:** The learner has a manageable way to begin and a reason to keep practicing.

## 13. Drafting and editing checklist

Before writing, identify the reader, the central idea, and what understanding it should change. Choose a familiar example and note any prerequisite or analogy limit the explanation needs.

Before delivering, check:

- [ ] The opening gives the learner a reason to care or something concrete to picture.
- [ ] Each section advances the central idea.
- [ ] The explanation includes the reasoning a beginner needs to follow it.
- [ ] Technical terms are introduced near examples that make them understandable.
- [ ] Examples demonstrate the point and remain consistent when reused.
- [ ] Analogies and simplifications do not imply a false mechanism.
- [ ] Real claims, quotations, dates, and statistics have appropriate support. Illustrations are identifiable as illustrations.
- [ ] The tone respects the learner and sounds natural aloud.
- [ ] Advice names a behavior and explains when it is useful.
- [ ] Humor and encouragement fit the subject.
- [ ] The lesson teaches its idea without requiring a TRY IT or LAB.
- [ ] The closing captures the lesson's earned insight.
