# Big Upside

We’ve said it many times: AI is a giant calculator running math at a scale nobody can picture. But that calculator can do some amazing things.

It solved a problem that had beaten biology for fifty years.

## The Protein Folding Problem

Your body runs on proteins. Think of them as tiny machines. They digest your food, carry oxygen, fight off infections, and build muscle. Each one starts as a long string of chemical beads, and then that string folds itself into a specific 3D shape in a fraction of a second.

**Teaching content:**

Here are the possible shapes.

One. Same string. A protein is a long string of chemical beads, called amino acids.

Two. Almost endless ways to fold. A medium protein can fold into more shapes than there are atoms in the universe.

Three. One shape determines what it does. Only the right shape fits the target and works. The target is the lock.

Function. Shape affects what a protein does. Some diseases involve proteins folding or behaving incorrectly. Many drugs work by binding to proteins and changing what they do.

Atoms in the universe: about ten to the eightieth, a one with eighty zeros. Possible shapes: vastly more, beyond imagination.

Proteins are built from 20 amino acids. The sequence determines the folding. The shape determines the function. Fifty years of lab work produced about 200,000 solved shapes, with hundreds of millions to go.

The same sequence folds into one working shape. Shape is everything.

## The Fifty-Year Problem

That’s the fifty-year problem in one picture. A protein’s shape determines what it does. Some diseases involve proteins folding or behaving incorrectly. Many drugs work by binding to proteins and changing what they do.

But a medium protein can fold more ways than there are atoms in the universe, so predicting shapes was brutal. Scientists had experimentally determined about 200,000 protein structures. Hundreds of millions of protein sequences still had no known structure.

## From Games to a Nobel Prize

### Board 2: Demis Hassabis: From Chess and Games to the Nobel Prize

**Image file:** `big-upside-hassabis-timeline.jpg`

![Demis Hassabis: From Chess and Games to the Nobel Prize](big-upside-hassabis-timeline.jpg)

**Teaching content:**

Demis Hassabis: from chess and games to the Nobel Prize.

1989: chess master, at age 13.

1994: co-designs Theme Park, at age 17.

2009: PhD in neuroscience, studying the brain.

2010: founds DeepMind, building an AI lab.

2020: AlphaFold solves protein folding, a fifty-year scientific problem.

2022: 200 million shapes, released free.

2024: Nobel Prize, in Chemistry.

A kid who loved games helped solve a fifty-year science problem.

## They Gave the Answers Away

For many proteins, AlphaFold predicted shapes with accuracy close to lab methods.

Then DeepMind did the part that actually mattered: they gave the answers away, free to everyone.

More than three million people in over 190 countries have used AlphaFold. In 2024, Hassabis won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.

When he won it, Hassabis said: “I’ve dedicated my career to advancing AI because of its unparalleled potential to improve the lives of billions of people.”
