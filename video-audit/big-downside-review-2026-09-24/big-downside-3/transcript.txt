[0:00.00-0:05.20] If your smartphone was about to get an amazing new feature, you would probably be excited.

[0:10.96-0:17.04] risk. The downside becomes clear through six ideas. The first idea is the black box.
[0:17.04-0:22.32] Traditional software runs on rules written line by line. But an AI model's behavior comes from
[0:22.32-0:27.68] billions of learned numerical patterns. If an AI suggests the name spot for your dog,
[0:27.68-0:32.64] no one, not even the engineers who designed it, can point to a specific spot inside the network
[0:32.64-0:38.32] and say, that's exactly where that decision came from. Because we cannot trace those internal pathways,
[0:38.32-0:43.52] fixing an AI is much harder than debugging ordinary software. Engineers can retrain the model or
[0:43.52-0:47.84] fine tune its outputs, but there is no complete repair manual that tells them exactly which
[0:47.84-0:53.28] internal part to change. To handle that unpredictability, we rely on the second idea, the challenge of
[0:53.28-0:58.80] guardrails. AI companies build protective layers into their models to block, redirect,
[0:58.80-1:04.48] or limit risky behavior. But no layer catches everything. This chart shows the challenge of
[1:04.48-1:11.20] scaling protections as systems become more capable. If AI changes itself, guardrails must keep up with

[1:17.76-1:22.40] surpasses people, the human rule setters could be less capable than the system they are trying
[1:22.40-1:28.16] to control. The worry grows as capability grows. Those are potential future risks.
[1:28.16-1:33.68] But here is a vulnerability we face right now. Jailbreaking. When someone intentionally wants

[1:56.32-2:01.52] official instructions from the AI company itself. Faced with conflicting directives,

[2:06.40-2:12.96] do not always have to break the rules to do damage. The fourth idea is bad actors. A scammer can take
