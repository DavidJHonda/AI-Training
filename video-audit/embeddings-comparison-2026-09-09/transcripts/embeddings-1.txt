[0.00–5.58]  How does an artificial intelligence actually understand what a word means using nothing but math?
[6.36–11.12]  You might already know that before an AI can read text, it breaks words down into tokens
[11.12–14.58]  and assigns each one a unique number called a token ID.
[15.46–20.28]  This graphic shows four student ID numbers. If you know a student's ID, it might let them
[20.28–24.86]  into the building, but it is just an identifier. The number doesn't tell you anything about
[24.86–29.46]  their personality. It won't tell you if they are funny, play hockey, or steal fries at lunch.
[29.46–35.30]  Token IDs work exactly the same way. They identify a piece of text, but they don't describe it.
[36.00–41.10]  Because an identifier only identifies, AI models require an entirely different system of numbers
[41.10–46.98]  to actually capture definitions and descriptions. This chart maps a taste test for Coke and coffee.
[47.56–51.98]  Rating six characteristics from 0 to 10, Coke scores high on sweet and fizz,
[52.26–57.32]  while coffee scores higher on bitter, heat, and caffeine. The entire row of numbers is a vector.
[57.88–63.22]  Each trait is a dimension, and the specific number is its value. If asked which drink scores
[63.22–68.66]  9 for sweet and 10 for fizz, you wouldn't need to guess. You know immediately it is Coke.
[69.16–74.04]  You have just used an ordered row of numbers as a precise mathematical profile for a real-world
[74.04–80.14]  item. Introducing a third drink, Pepsi, creates a problem. Looking at the original six dimensions,
[80.70–85.96]  Pepsi scores exactly the same as Coke. The data cannot tell them apart. To separate them,
[85.96–92.18]  we add a seventh dimension, citrus. Now we see a difference. Coke scores a 1, while Pepsi scores
[92.18–97.50]  a 10. When meanings overlap, adding a new mathematical dimension allows the profile to
[97.50–102.30]  capture subtle differences that the earlier metrics completely missed. This table compares
[102.30–107.56]  our simple taste test to how an AI builds internal profiles. Instead of three drinks,
[107.90–112.02]  the AI evaluates every token in its vocabulary across thousands of dimensions.
[112.58–116.78]  While we chose simple numbers, the AI independently learns complex,
[117.08–122.14]  positive, negative, and decimal numbers. Importantly, these dimensions don't have
[122.14–127.74]  human labels. They capture abstract patterns in token usage. Despite the massive scale
[127.74–131.68]  and the lack of familiar labels, the underlying mechanism is identical.
[132.34–136.78]  Both systems use an ordered row of numbers to mathematically describe what something is.
[137.36–140.84]  Let's bring all this together and look at a diagram of a real embedding table.
[140.84–146.14]  When you type the word CAT into an AI, here is exactly what happens mathematically.
[146.80–153.32]  First, the text CAT is converted to its token ID, in this case 4719. The system uses that
[153.32–158.80]  number as a lookup address to find its specific row in the table. Inside that row, the columns
[158.80–170.82]  marked D1, D2, and so on represent the abstract dimensions. Each position holds a specific
[170.82–175.90]  completely assembled row of learned numbers, you're looking at the token's embedding.
[176.26–181.70]  This system is so robust that even fragments of words get their own profiles. If you input
[181.70–187.90]  unbelievable, the AI splits it into UN, BELIE, and VABEL, and each of those fragments receives
[187.90–193.06]  its own unique embedding. AI uses numbers to work with meaning. Those numbers help
[193.06–195.24]  AI recognize similarities and differences.
