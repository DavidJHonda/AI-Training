# Shorter core course proposal

**Draft for discussion · Be Smarter Than the Tool · October 12, 2026**

Make a 15-lesson core the default course, with a complete finish of its own. Keep the existing curriculum available as the full course and as optional extensions. The core should help a high school beginner **use AI to learn and do useful work, judge its answers, and recognize when to step back**.

This is a proposed syllabus and editorial plan. The current course, navigation, videos, and completion requirements remain unchanged. The [destination map](core-course-destination-map-2026-10-12.md) accounts for all 59 currently routed pages, including openers and utilities.

## What finishing should mean

A student who finishes should be able to choose a suitable task, give AI useful instructions and material, improve an answer, check important claims against evidence, and take responsibility for the result. They should also recognize when apparent agreement, caring language, or convincing media deserves a different response, and know when to involve a real person.

They do not need to explain transformer architecture, survey the future of work, or complete a career exploration project to demonstrate those outcomes. Those subjects remain valuable destinations after the core.

The proposed audience stays roughly age 16, with little assumed technical knowledge. The default experience is self-paced, with optional group discussion. A teacher can use the full curriculum or add extensions without making them requirements for everyone.

## Length and practice budget

Use **four hours including initial setup, teaching, required practice, and the finish** as an initial design ceiling. This is a constraint for the prototype, not a prediction of how long the existing material takes. Confirm or change it after observing students; do not publish it as a course duration yet. Breaks, optional extensions, and group discussions would add time.

There are 15 required lessons including the finish. A brief welcome and tool setup sit at the beginning of lesson 1. Section introductions become short orientation within lessons rather than additional completion steps. Students watch or read an explanation; they are not assigned both.

Protect three substantial hands-on experiences: studying from a short source, briefing AI on a task, and making a checked recommendation. Other lessons use brief decisions, comparisons, or revisions. Reuse examples where it helps. Do not attach the full original activities to every condensed lesson.

Use one accessible chat tool throughout the core. Provide text that can be pasted as an alternative to uploads, and sample exchanges for tool-access interruptions. A sample exchange supports practice but does not count as independently using AI; students should get a supported opportunity to complete that part. The dedicated notebook workflow and app feature tours remain optional.

## Proposed core syllabus

Lesson numbers below identify proposed destinations. Existing titles are retained where their teaching job remains the same. New combinations need new introductions and shorter explanations, not several existing lessons placed end to end.

### Get oriented and use it

| Lesson | What it teaches | Required practice and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. What Is AI?** | Brief welcome and setup; familiar AI uses; app versus model; learning patterns during training and generating an answer. Explain that fluent output does not establish truth or humanlike understanding. | Choose which of two short explanations describes a generated answer and explain why confidence does not prove correctness. Make a harmless first request during setup. |
| **2. Where AI Works Best** | Choose an appropriate task. Keep the current practical strengths, one contrast with ordinary software, and the reason your own knowledge matters. Include a brief distinction between getting an answer and permitting a tool to act. | Choose AI, another tool, or a person for three tasks and justify one choice. Identify what would need checking before allowing an action. |
| **3. Honesty & Privacy** | Follow the assignment’s rules, own submitted work, share only relevant information, and consider other people affected by sharing. | Clean up one prompt containing unnecessary private details; decide what assistance is allowed in a supplied classroom scenario. |
| **4. Learn with AI** | Use supplied material to practice retrieval and understanding. Answer before seeing the solution, then check the original passage. | **Hands-on experience 1:** use a short supplied text to generate three questions, answer them, check one answer against the text, and explain one idea without AI. |

### Get better results

| Lesson | What it teaches | Required practice and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **5. Questions Matter** | Frame the actual problem and leave room for an answer you did not expect. Preserve the distinction between deciding what to ask and writing instructions. | Rewrite a leading question. Compare supplied responses, then explain what the better question made possible. A live comparison is optional. |
| **6. Prompting Matters** | Supply the situation, material, desired output, and an appropriate next step. Use only the detail the task needs. | **Hands-on experience 2:** improve a weak brief for a small club event using supplied notes; compare the result with the constraints. Preserve Build a Rubric as optional practice. |
| **7. Context Matters** | Understand what information is available for an answer, notice missing material, and separate unrelated tasks. App memory and projects receive a brief explanation, not a setup tour. | In the club-event example, identify a missing constraint and revise the context. Explain why an unshared file cannot support the answer. |
| **8. Evaluation Matters** | Read, understand, validate; decide how closely to check; use, fix, or walk away. Distinguish factual correctness from usefulness. | Judge a low-stakes draft and a consequential factual answer. Choose the appropriate check and explain why. Introduce one example of missing or unrepresentative evidence. |

### Recognize the traps

| Lesson | What it teaches | Required practice and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **9. Check the Claim and the Source** | Distinguish an invented claim from a real document used incompletely. Teach a single verification routine: identify the claim, find the original, check support and relevant exceptions. | Compare the existing invented-study example with the basketball-rulebook exception. Locate the decisive passage and explain why asking AI to repeat its answer is insufficient. |
| **10. Flattery Trap** | Friendly agreement is not an independent assessment. Ask for criticism anchored in the work and criteria. | Adapt Make Feedback Useful: replace a request for praise with a request for evidence-based criticism, then choose the feedback worth acting on. |
| **11. Support Trap** | Distinguish useful venting, preparation for real action, and situations requiring a person. Explain why a human-sounding response can invite misplaced trust; include a stopping point for unproductive conversation. | Keep three contrasting support scenarios: cooling down, preparing a real conversation, and getting help from a person. State the next action rather than merely naming the trap. |
| **12. Fake Trap** | Appearance and plausibility are insufficient evidence. Verify consequential media or messages through an independent source or channel. | Use one photo pair as the reveal, then decide how to verify a school-closure claim. The verification decision is the completion task. |

### Apply it and finish

| Lesson | What it teaches | Required practice and evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **13. Improve the Result** | Treat the first answer as a draft. Bring an idea of your own, request a useful alternative, and choose a revision using criteria. | Return to the club-event plan. Propose one improvement before asking AI, compare alternatives, and explain what to keep. Use a supplied response if necessary. |
| **14. Put AI to Work** | Combine briefing, source checking, revision, and a human decision in one bounded task. | **Hands-on experience 3:** adapt Turn Reviews into Recommendations. Use a smaller, clearly fictional review set with a common issue and an important exception. Check two claims against specific reviews, choose one recommendation, and record what you changed or rejected and why. Finish with a short recommendation, not a generated slide deck. |
| **15. Finish Smarter** | Consolidate the core habits, demonstrate transfer, and choose a realistic next use. | A proposed ten-question scenario check plus the work from lesson 14. Keep explanations and retries. Finish with one sentence naming what the student will try next and what they will check. |

## What the combinations preserve and remove

**Lesson 1 combines introductory material.** Keep one compact explanation of training and generation, one familiar example, and the distinction between fluent behavior and human understanding. The full philosophy discussion and technical mechanism sequence remain optional. This must not become three introductory lessons inside one page.

**Lesson 9 combines two source problems because the student performs the same verification routine.** Retain the distinction between a source that does not exist and a source whose exception was missed. Use the fake-study and rulebook examples only. Move the extra hallucination examples, retrieval mechanics, and coding lab out of this required lesson. If students cannot explain both errors after the prototype, split this into two short lessons and remove equivalent work elsewhere rather than rushing the distinction.

**Lesson 11 is centered on Support Trap.** Borrow only the minimum explanation of misplaced personal trust from Mind Trap and a concrete stopping rule from Engagement Trap. Their full lessons and separate activities remain optional. Preserve the existing nuance that some venting can be useful; do not teach that all emotional use is the same.

**Lesson 13 borrows methods from Next Level Moves and Creative Thinking.** It practices one cycle of improvement using familiar material. The full creativity lesson, career plan, roleplay, forecasts, and 30-day action plan stay together in the career extension.

Honesty & Privacy moves before the first source-based activity. Lessons 4 and 6 include an immediate check of the result even though the complete evaluation framework comes later. Safety and checking are taught at the moment of use, then developed further.

## Where the rest goes

Provide two clear entry choices eventually: **Core course** and **Full course**. A core student can finish without opening an extension. The full route keeps its breadth and existing sequence unless separately revised.

After the core, offer three coherent extensions:

| Extension | Content and reason to take it |
| --- | --- |
| **Understand AI** | Preserve the technical sequence from Training through The Next Token, with its opener and recurring examples. For students who want to understand how words become answers. |
| **AI and the Future** | Preserve the Embrace the Future sequence. Include the full Where’s the Line? discussion as an optional companion. For classes exploring benefits, risks, incentives, ethics, and changing work. |
| **Build Your Skills** | Preserve the Career Explorer chain beginning in Next Level Moves and continuing through People Skills, Creative Thinking, Curious & Flexible, and Make Your Move. Add a short entry recap for students arriving directly from the core. |

Use **Go deeper** links for a specific nearby question: Does AI Think?, Training Bias, the full Document Trap, app setup guidance, and fuller trap explanations. These links lead to existing material; they do not create another required checklist.

Keep the coding experiences available as an optional practice sequence: project setup → Dino game → 2048. It should not require a learner to complete the future or technical sequence merely to build a game. Preserve the current full-course activities while adding a clear way to enter this optional sequence.

## Completion and assessment

The core completion indicator counts only the 15 core lessons. Orientation, resources, reviews, group exercises, and optional material do not add required progress. The full course retains its own completion record, and taking the core does not automatically award completion of the full curriculum.

For the proposed ten-question check, use two questions on task fit and the basic model, two on questions and context, three on checking sources and misleading output, two on privacy and interpersonal judgment, and one on revision and responsibility. No item should require optional technical or future material. Keep the current 80% quiz threshold as a prototype setting, with explanations and retries linked to the corresponding core lesson.

The practical evidence from lesson 14 should show the student can identify supporting passages, explain a rejected or revised answer, and justify a recommendation. In a class, a teacher can review it. In self-paced use, provide a worked comparison and a short self-review. A completion certificate should describe course completion; an automated quiz plus self-review does not independently certify practical mastery.

Do not add a career capstone after this finish. The student's next real-world action is an invitation, not another condition for completing the core.

## How to review and test this proposal

The main editorial decisions are the narrow practical promise, the 15-lesson route, the source-checking combination in lesson 9, and moving the future and career sequences out of required completion. The [complete destination map](core-course-destination-map-2026-10-12.md) makes the consequences visible lesson by lesson.

After agreement on the syllabus, draft text versions of the combined lessons and the three hands-on activities. Test with a few students near the intended age. Record setup time separately from teaching and practice, and observe both a typical experience and the longest uninterrupted attempt. Do not shorten instructions just to meet the clock when students need them.

Ask students to explain the job of each lesson and perform a new version of its task. Pay particular attention to whether they distinguish invented sources from missing exceptions, useful feedback from flattery, and preparation from real-world action. If they can recite the rule but cannot perform the check, revise the practice.

If the prototype exceeds the four-hour ceiling, remove secondary examples, feature setup, or repeated exercises before cutting the three substantial practice experiences. If the promised outcomes still require longer, revisit the scope or ceiling explicitly. The shorter course must earn its completion point through demonstrated use and judgment.
