[0.00–6.14]  When you feed text into a large language model, the system first chops those words into smaller
[6.14–12.82]  chunks called tokens. Each token is then assigned a unique token ID. How does the AI actually
[12.82–18.88]  know what the underlying word means? These four student ID cards illustrate a limitation
[18.88–27.00]  of identifiers. Numbers like 1024 or 2048 identify exactly who a student is, but describe
[27.00–33.16]  nothing about them. The ID grants access, but fails to tell us if that person is funny,
[33.16–39.52]  plays hockey, or steals fries at lunch. To an AI, a token ID is essentially just a catalog
[39.52–44.88]  number. A completely different system is required to describe a token's actual characteristics
[44.88–49.84]  and capture its meaning. We can describe characteristics using numbers
[49.84–55.32]  by looking at a taste test comparing Coke and coffee. Here, we rate them on six specific
[55.32–61.54]  characteristics using a scale from 0 to 10. Looking at the extremes in the data, Coke
[61.54–70.78]  scores a 9 in sweet and a 10 in fizz, while coffee scores a 9 in bitter and an 8 in caffeine.
[70.78–76.80]  These numbers create a distinct mathematical profile for each drink. In data science,
[76.80–82.80]  this entire row of numbers is called a vector. Each specific position across the top, like
[82.80–88.96]  sweet or bitter, is a dimension, and the specific number sitting in that spot is its value.
[88.96–95.66]  Now, we run into a problem. If we add a third drink, Pepsi, to our taste test, it scores
[95.66–101.76]  exactly the same as Coke on all six of our original dimensions. If our six numbers are
[101.76–107.52]  identical, how can we mathematically tell Coke and Pepsi apart? The solution is to
[107.52–114.72]  add a seventh dimension, citrus. Pepsi scores a 10 here, while Coke scores a 1. Adding this
[114.72–120.16]  new column allows the numerical profile to capture a subtle difference the original six missed.
[120.16–127.36]  Artificial intelligence uses this exact same concept to understand text. This chart compares
[127.36–132.80]  a human taste test directly to AI token mechanics. While our taste test only mapped
[132.80–139.12]  three specific drinks to rows, an AI creates a row for every single token in its entire vocabulary.
[139.68–144.56]  And the AI doesn't stop at seven dimensions. It typically utilizes thousands of separate
[144.56–150.64]  dimensions to capture the deep complexity of human language. Instead of simple 0 to 10 ratings,
[150.64–155.60]  the AI learns highly specific positive and negative decimal numbers during its training phase.
[156.24–161.44]  Unlike our taste test, AI dimensions do not have human labels like sweetness or fizz.
[162.00–166.00]  The values capture hidden patterns in how words are used alongside one another.
[166.56–169.92]  These unlabeled values work together to represent meaning.
[169.92–173.84]  In AI, this complete row of numbers is called an embedding.
[175.44–180.56]  This diagram takes us inside a real model to see how this works in practice,
[180.56–186.88]  starting with a user typing the word cat into a prompt. The word cat is assigned token ID
[186.96–194.48]  4719. The AI uses this ID purely as an address to locate its specific row inside a massive
[194.48–201.76]  database called an embedding table. The columns labeled D1, D2, and so on represent the thousands
[201.76–208.32]  of dimensions. Each cell contains a learned value, which in AI terminology is called a parameter.
[209.04–214.72]  This single complete row of parameters is the token's embedding. Every piece of text the
[214.72–221.36]  model processes receives this treatment. Word fragments like un, belie, and vable each have
[221.36–227.84]  their own unique token IDs and dedicated rows of numbers in the table. AI uses numbers to
[227.84–234.72]  work with meaning. Those numbers help AI recognize similarities and differences.
