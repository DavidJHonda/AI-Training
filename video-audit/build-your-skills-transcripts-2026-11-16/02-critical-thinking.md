**[0:00]** Look at the left side. Knowledge forms your baseline. It helps you understand a subject and recognize when something in a text or a prompt doesn't add up. On the right side is the active component. Critical thinking means you question a claim, examine the evidence, and consider alternative explanations before you decide what to do next. When you combine these two halves, the result is better questions and better decisions. Be smarter than the tool. It isn't don't trust anything either. It's the habit of asking what would have to be true for a claim to hold up.

**[0:35]** In 2015, news outlets around the world published stories about a scientific study claiming a delicious new method for losing weight. One front page ran the headline, Slim by Chocolate. The face value reaction was immediate. Sounds great. I believe it, but a critical thinking reaction takes a different path. Wait, what's behind the claim? You must force yourself to pause, especially when a claim matches what you want to believe. The study itself actually existed. However, it was deliberately designed to be scientifically flimsy. This diagram illustrates the primary flaw in the data. The researchers used a tiny sample size of only 15 participants, but they tracked 18 entirely different measurements for each person.

**[1:23]** With that many measurements in such a small group, a chance result could look like an important discovery. The supposed research institute backing the study was nothing more than a fabricated website. The true author was a journalist. He orchestrated the entire stunt to prove that bad science wrapped in a highly appealing headline will spread globally before anyone verifies the facts. Critical thinking requires the ability to read between those lines, Particularly, when our desires make us vulnerable to confirmation bias.

**[1:55]** This board outlines five habits you can build to sharpen your thinking. Every single one functions as a direct question. Habit one, is it actually right? You check what evidence supports the claim and verify if that evidence genuinely aligns with the conclusion. Habit two, do I know enough to judge? You have to recognize the limits of your own knowledge and identify exactly what you still need to learn. Habit 3, what's missing? You actively hunt for omitted information or alternative explanations. Fourth, why am I convinced? Question your own motives to see if you are swayed by hard evidence, confident wording, or wishful thinking. And fifth, what's my call? Finally, you decide what to believe or do, keeping the flexibility to change your mind when you learn more.

**[2:43]** These five questions work on any information you consume. They are especially critical when you use artificial intelligence. AI can help you find a flaw in your thinking or confidently repeat it. You need to judge which is happening. AI amplifies whatever you bring to it. Good thinking in, sharper output.
