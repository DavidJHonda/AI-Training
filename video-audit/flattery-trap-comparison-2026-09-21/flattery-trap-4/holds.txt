== HOLD 2:45.83 -> 3:38.77  (52.9s)
   narration: This table outlines five ways to fight the flattery trap. By giving the AI a clearer job and a standard to measure against, we prevent it from simply agreeing with us. The first move is ask don't tell. If you say I think this plan is good, thoughts, you hand the AI your opinion to echo. Instead, hold back your verdict. A better prompt is evaluate this plan before I tell you what I think. The neutral question gives the AI less of your own confidence to reflect back at you. The second move is ask for the gaps. Asking is my essay good is a broad request for approval, and a cheerful yes doesn't help you revise. Ask specifically, what's weak, what's missing, and what would someone who disagrees say. Now you have given the AI specific targets to locate. The third move is use a rubric.

== HOLD 3:38.93 -> 4:32.40  (53.5s)
   narration: The third move is use a rubric. If you just say grade my essay, the AI might use criteria completely different from your teachers. Instead, tell it graded against this rubric. Quote the evidence and show how to move up one level. Keep in mind, asking it to quote evidence ties the feedback to your actual writing, but the response is still just a guide. It is something to check against your work, not an official guarantee of accuracy or a final grade. The fourth move is argue the other side. Asking don't you agree invites the system to side with the position you just shared. Instead, instruct it to give me the three strongest counter arguments and explain why someone might hold them. There is one important limit here. If the AI fails to produce a strong counter argument, that does not prove your position is flawless. It might simply have struggled with the task. You are still the final judge of the arguments it provides. Each of these moves works because you are replacing a request for validation with a concrete standard for the AI to meet.
