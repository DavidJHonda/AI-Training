# Business Opportunity Research: A Broader AI Training Company

**Prepared:** August 19, 2026  
**Status:** Working business case for discussion  
**Scope:** A new AI training business built around existing company capabilities, including but not limited to **Be Smarter Than the Tool**

## Executive summary

There is a credible business here, and the company appears unusually well positioned to test it.

The opportunity is not simply to sell the current high-school course. It is to create a broader, vendor-neutral AI education division that reuses the training, certification, support, and team infrastructure already operating at QuickBooksTraining.com. **Be Smarter Than the Tool** can become the youth and family flagship within that larger business, while professional workshops and team training provide the faster path to revenue.

The strongest reason to proceed is not that AI training is fashionable. It is the combination of:

- a large existing base of learners and business customers
- 28 years of training operations and instructional credibility
- an existing platform for live classes, self-paced courses, certification, help, team seats, and progress reporting
- two beginner AI courses already included in Training Plans, one of which also supports AI certification
- a substantial new course asset that teaches durable AI literacy, judgment, and safe use

The strongest reason to be careful is that generic AI instruction is already cheap or free. Google sells AI Essentials for $49, Code.org offers a free high-school AI curriculum, and specialist companies are rapidly adding courses. A new business will not win by offering another collection of prompt tips. It needs to win on **trusted instruction, real work outcomes, live help, credible assessment, and safe use**.

### Recommendation

Proceed, but treat the next 90 days as paid market validation rather than a full business launch.

1. Audit the two existing AI courses before defining another professional course, so the new business has a clear boundary and does not duplicate Training Plan value.
2. Launch one live, vendor-neutral beta for small-business teams that focuses on cross-functional workflows, governance, and adoption rather than QuickBooks features.
3. Use current AI-course learners as a discovery and recruitment audience, then offer a private-team version to test higher-value B2B demand.
4. Keep the new customer-facing brand on a separate URL while reusing the QuickBooks Training platform and operations behind the scenes.
5. Develop the youth line in parallel, but do not publicly scale it until the data model, vendor age rules, parent consent, recordings, and instructor safeguards are resolved.

The first professional beta should be sold before a large catalog, new LMS, membership community, or custom software is built.

This should be built as a **separate AI training business powered by existing training capabilities**, not as a one-course website or an extension of the QuickBooks Training Plan.

The shared promise across these branches could be expressed as:

> Learn to use AI for real work without giving up judgment, accuracy, privacy, or responsibility.

That promise is broad enough to survive changes in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and whatever comes next. It also grows directly from the current course's central idea: the person remains smarter than the tool.

## Market evidence

### AI use is widespread, but the numbers require careful interpretation

Different reputable surveys report very different adoption rates because they define “AI use” differently.

- The U.S. Census Bureau's nationally representative Business Trends and Outlook Survey found that 17% to 20% of businesses used AI in any business function between December 2025 and May 2026. Use was higher among large firms and below 20% among firms with four or fewer employees. ([U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html))
- Intuit's 2026 AI Impact Report, using more than 34,000 survey responses plus anonymized payment data from 5.3 million QuickBooks businesses, reported that 77% of U.S. small and midsize businesses used AI “regularly.” That measure includes free tools and AI features embedded inside other products. Only 12% of U.S. businesses with observed payment records had paid for dedicated AI tools between 2021 and 2025. ([Intuit 2026 AI Impact Report](https://www.intuit.com/blog/global-stories/ai-impact-report/))
- Gallup found that, as of May 2026, 52% of U.S. employees used AI at work at least a few times a year, 30% used it a few times a week, and 15% used it daily. Only 25% said their organization had communicated a clear AI plan. ([Gallup AI indicator](https://www.gallup.com/699797/indicator-artificial-intelligence.aspx))

The apparent conflict is useful. Many people touch an AI feature, but far fewer have integrated a deliberate, paid, repeatable AI practice. The business opportunity is not basic exposure. It is moving customers from experimentation to safe, useful workflows.

### There is measurable value, but not automatic transformation

The latest Census worker survey found that about 55% of workers had used AI for at least one of 11 work tasks. Among people who used it in the prior week, 31% estimated that it saved one to two hours, while 10% said it saved no time and 3% said it required additional time. Common uses included information search, writing, idea generation, summarization, and administration. ([U.S. Census Bureau, August 2026](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2026/08/ai-use-at-work.html))

Gallup reported that 65% of employees in organizations that had implemented AI described a positive productivity effect, but only 14% strongly agreed that AI had transformed how work gets done. Manager support was strongly associated with adoption and perceived value. ([Gallup AI indicator](https://www.gallup.com/699797/indicator-artificial-intelligence.aspx))

SHRM's 2025 survey of 1,812 U.S. workers found that 77% of AI users said it helped them accomplish more in less time and 73% said it improved work quality. More than half identified enhanced training as the top priority for better AI outcomes. The sample was an unweighted online panel, so it is useful directional evidence rather than a national prevalence estimate. ([SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-report-warns-of-widening-skills-gap-as-ai-adoption-reaches-))

These findings support training that is based on workflows, judgment, and management support. They do not support a promise that buying a course automatically transforms a company.

### The accounting and professional-services wedge is especially attractive

The 2026 Thomson Reuters Future of Professionals report surveyed 1,816 professionals in law, tax, audit, accounting, compliance, risk, and global trade. It reported that 74% used AI several times a week, 41% lacked access to professional-grade tools, and 34% used unsanctioned tools. In organizations with a named AI strategy, 66% said AI met or exceeded value expectations, versus 22% where no active strategy existed. ([Thomson Reuters Future of Professionals 2026](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute/future-of-professionals-2026/report))

Intuit's 2026 accountant-facing interpretation of its small-business research argues that the client conversation has shifted from whether clients use AI to where they use it, what information it touches, who reviews it, and how accountability is maintained. ([Intuit Firm of the Future](https://www.firmofthefuture.com/artificial-intelligence/ai-impact-report-2026/))

This aligns closely with the company's existing audience and suggests a defensible position: **practical AI for financially sensitive, client-facing work where accuracy and human review matter**.

### Youth demand is real, but so is the safety gap

Common Sense Media's nationally representative 2026 survey of 1,204 children ages 9 to 17 found that 86% used AI and nearly a quarter used it daily. Among AI users, 85% used it for schoolwork or homework and 75% used it to create things. Three-quarters said their school had discussed usage rules, but only just over half had been taught to use AI safely. More than four in 10 said no parent or guardian had discussed AI safety with them. ([Common Sense Media census](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/a-comprehensive-report-on-teens-tweens-and-ai), [report PDF](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/2026-ai-use-by-tweens-and-teens-1.pdf))

A separate nationally representative 2025 survey found that more than a third of teens had been misled by fake online content and nearly three-quarters supported stronger privacy protections, content warnings, and labels for AI-generated content. ([Common Sense Media trust research](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/research-brief-teens-trust-and-technology-in-the-age-of-ai))

This is strong evidence for the problem **Be Smarter Than the Tool** addresses. It does not by itself prove that families will pay a particular price.

## Proposed offer architecture

### 1. Professional foundation and product boundary

Keep both existing AI courses inside the core Training Plan as the QuickBooks-specific foundation:

- **Improve Your Bookkeeping with AI** owns QuickBooks AI agents and certification preparation.
- **Practical AI for QuickBooks Users** owns practical beginner AI education framed specifically for QuickBooks users; its full curriculum should be mapped before a new professional syllabus is approved.

The separate business should add vendor-neutral and organizational capability beyond that foundation:

- what modern AI can and cannot do
- choosing tools and models
- prompt and context design
- research, source checking, and document work
- spreadsheet and data analysis
- client communication and SOP creation
- privacy, confidential information, and approved-use boundaries
- human review, escalation, and accountability
- repeatable workflows and simple agents

Before building, create a one-page content map for both current courses. Mark every proposed new lesson as **already covered**, **natural Training Plan extension**, or **distinct new-business value**. Only the third category belongs in the first separate-brand offer.

### 2. First new paid workshop

**Working concept:** *AI Workflow and Governance Lab for Small-Business Teams*

**Format:** One 3.5-hour live online workshop, capped at 25 to 30 learners, with an instructor and TA.

**Beta price:** $199 to $299.  
**Target standard price after proof:** $399 to $599.  
**Team option:** Group seats and a private version starting around $5,000.

**What each learner should leave with:**

1. A one-page safe-use policy for their work.
2. A map of one recurring workflow worth improving.
3. A tested AI-assisted version of that workflow.
4. A verification checklist for accuracy, confidentiality, and approval.
5. A reusable prompt, template, or lightweight agent.
6. A 30-day adoption plan with one measurable time or quality outcome.

Examples should use cross-functional work the audience recognizes: turning notes into a clean SOP, comparing documents, drafting careful customer communication, researching a decision with sources, creating an internal safe-use rule, handing an AI-assisted task between employee and manager, and building a review checklist. Real customer or client data should not be required in class.

This workshop must be differentiated from **both** existing classes. It should teach **cross-tool team practice, governance, implementation, and measurable adoption**, while the Training Plan courses remain the place for QuickBooks-centered individual learning. The final title and syllabus should be approved only after the course-overlap audit.

### 3. Professional product ladder

If the beta works, the offer can expand in this order:

1. Free 45-minute briefing or diagnostic webinar.
2. Paid half-day outcome workshop.
3. Four- to six-week applied cohort with a capstone workflow.
4. Role-based courses for owners, bookkeepers, operations, sales, and managers.
5. Team seat bundles and private workshops.
6. Monthly office hours or one-on-one AI workflow help.
7. Advanced workflow reviews, policy development, and advisory.

The business should earn the right to move from one level to the next. A catalog should be the result of repeated customer requests, not the starting assumption.

### 4. Youth and family product line

**Be Smarter Than the Tool** should remain the flagship intellectual property for youth AI literacy. The current course contains 55 lessons across seven sections and is better suited to a complete self-paced or hybrid experience than to four densely packed live sessions.

A live family cohort should be a curated experience drawn from the full course, not the whole course read aloud. One possible structure is:

| Session | Focus | Visible outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | What AI is, how it answers, and why confidence is not understanding | Student can explain the basic model in plain language |
| 2 | Questions, prompting, context, and evaluation | Student improves a weak result and explains why it improved |
| 3 | Hallucinations, bias, privacy, manipulation, and fakes | Student completes a personal safety and verification checklist |
| 4 | Future work, human skills, and responsible use | Student presents a personal AI-use plan or small project |

A parent orientation and a family closing session are sensible. A mailed diploma is optional. The more important proof is a student-created artifact, a short presentation, and a before-and-after measure of judgment.

The youth line should have a separate landing path, parent-facing checkout, and language that emphasizes literacy and judgment rather than fear, cheating, or guaranteed career advantage.

## Brand and URL strategy

A separate URL is the confirmed direction.

QuickBooksTraining.com has a precise promise centered on its Training Plan and carries a prominent Intuit endorsement. A broad AI site will discuss several vendors, serve non-QuickBooks audiences, and eventually include youth offerings. Keeping the customer-facing brands separate protects the focus of the core Training Plan, reduces confusion about what Intuit has endorsed, and gives the new business room to grow.

The recommended architecture is:

- Intuit QuickBooks Training continues to operate the core Training Plan at QuickBooksTraining.com.
- A new vendor-neutral AI brand and URL houses professional, team, and youth paths.
- The existing platform, staff, and operations are reused where practical.
- Whether the public brand discloses a relationship to QuickBooksTraining.com should be tested separately and remains subject to contract and trademark review.

Before cross-promotion or public claims, review the Intuit agreement for restrictions on adjacent products, endorsements, customer data, marks, and co-branding. Do not imply that Intuit endorses the broader AI catalog unless it explicitly does.

## Go-to-market plan

### Start with customer discovery, then a paid presale

The first market test should use actual purchasing behavior, not only a survey.

1. Interview 12 to 15 recent customers across solo bookkeepers, small-business owners, and team managers.
2. Ask about recurring work, current AI tools, confidential data, failed experiments, company policies, and what outcome would justify paying for live help.
3. Send a short interest survey to a recent, permissioned customer segment.
4. Build a plain landing page for one workshop with a date, curriculum, instructor, outcome, price, and refund policy.
5. Ask for payment or a refundable deposit.

Useful discovery questions include:

- What AI task have you tried more than once but not made reliable?
- Where are you worried about client or financial information?
- Which repetitive task costs the most time each week?
- What would you need to show a manager or client before using AI in that task?
- Would you rather attend a public workshop, bring a team, or get one-on-one help?
- What result would make a $299 or $499 workshop worthwhile?

### Distribution sequence

1. Learners in either current AI course who also identify as owners, managers, or team leads, subject to communication permissions.
2. Team-plan administrators and group-training leads.
3. The broader permissioned QuickBooksTraining.com audience.
4. Accountant and bookkeeping associations, newsletters, and webinars.
5. Paid acquisition only after landing-page conversion and contribution economics are known.

For the youth line, the channel is different: parents, local schools, homeschool groups, community organizations, libraries, and parent referrals. The adult customer list may contain parents, but that does not automatically make it an efficient or permitted youth marketing list.

## Operating model and technology

### Reuse the mature platform first

The professional beta should use the existing company stack for registration, payment, live delivery, recordings, assessments, support, and team administration. A new brand does not require a new operating system.

The current **Be Smarter Than the Tool** prototype is a single-file React web app deployed through Vercel. It stores progress locally in the browser and currently sends first name, last name, email, city, and state to a Google Apps Script endpoint. That is sufficient for testing with known students. It is not yet the ideal account, consent, progress, or data architecture for a public youth product.

For a production youth version:

- the paying customer record should belong to the parent or guardian
- the learner should receive a random internal ID
- child email should not be required by default
- collect only the minimum child information needed, such as first name or display name and age band
- progress should sync to the established LMS only when the privacy model is approved
- provide clear parent access, correction, deletion, and retention controls
- separate learning analytics from marketing data
- create a written incident response and deletion procedure

### Content maintenance

AI course content decays faster than traditional software training because both product features and public understanding change quickly. Every lesson should be assigned to one of three maintenance classes:

- **Durable:** judgment, verification, critical thinking, privacy habits, how language models work.
- **Reviewed periodically:** capability comparisons, model selection, workplace patterns, energy use.
- **Fast-changing lab:** specific interfaces, pricing, vendor features, account setup, and tool workflows.

Fast-changing labs should be modular and dated so they can be replaced without rebuilding the durable course.

## Youth privacy, safety, and vendor access

This section identifies launch requirements, not legal conclusions. Counsel familiar with education technology and children's privacy should review the final design.

### COPPA and student privacy

COPPA applies to certain sites and online services that collect personal information from children under 13. The FTC's 2025 rule changes reinforce verifiable parental consent, separate consent for certain third-party disclosures, data minimization, and purpose-limited retention. A target audience of 14 to 17 reduces direct COPPA exposure, but it does not eliminate state privacy laws, contract terms, safeguarding duties, or parent expectations. ([FTC COPPA update](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-finalizes-changes-childrens-privacy-rule-limiting-companies-ability-monetize-kids-data))

If the product is sold through schools, FERPA becomes relevant when the service receives personally identifiable information from education records. The U.S. Department of Education says a vendor operating under the school-official exception must perform a school function, remain under school control for the use and maintenance of the data, use it only for authorized purposes, and avoid unauthorized redisclosure. The Department also recommends collecting the minimum necessary data, maintaining security and breach procedures, and planning for destruction. ([Department of Education FAQ](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/i-want-use-online-tool-or-application-part-my-course-however-i-am-worried-it-violation-ferpa), [vendor guidance PDF](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/sites/default/files/resource_document/file/Vendor%20FAQ.pdf))

### AI vendor age rules

Vendor rules must be designed into the course rather than handled as a footnote.

- OpenAI's consumer terms require users to be at least 13 or the local age of digital consent; users under 18 need parent or guardian permission. The same terms prohibit sharing account credentials. ([OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/policies/terms-of-use/))
- Anthropic says its consumer Claude product is available only to people over 18 and may require age verification. ([Claude minimum age](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/13117299-minimum-age-requirement-access-restriction), [age assurance](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/15171100-age-assurance-on-claude))
- NotebookLM is generally available to personal-account users above the applicable age of consent, while Workspace for Education can make it available to users of all ages when enabled by the administrator. Some features remain restricted for users under 18. ([Google NotebookLM help](https://support.google.com/notebooklm/answer/16164461?hl=en))

AdVenture handles Claude's age rule by requiring the parent to own the account and remain nearby while the child operates it. That is a visible safety measure, but it should not be assumed to resolve every vendor term or account-sharing question. Obtain written vendor or legal confirmation before adopting a similar model.

Safer youth designs include:

- tool-agnostic course simulations that require no child AI account
- approved education accounts managed by a school
- a company-controlled application using a commercial API and explicit parental consent, if vendor terms permit it
- parent-led demonstrations where the parent remains the actual operator
- optional labs that list age requirements and permitted alternatives

### Operational safeguards for live youth cohorts

- parent or guardian is the purchaser and primary contact
- explicit consent for participation, recordings, project publication, and testimonials
- camera-off default and no expectation that a child disclose a last name, school, exact location, or personal account
- at least two trained adults in live sessions
- instructor background checks and a written communication policy
- no private instructor-to-student direct messages outside approved systems
- defined reporting and escalation procedure
- no public project links that expose a child's identity or private data
- recordings distributed only under a carefully reviewed consent and access model
- defined deletion date rather than “kept forever”

The right market-sizing model is bottom-up. Let:

> Reachable qualified audience × purchase conversion × price = gross launch revenue

| Illustrative reachable audience | Conversion | Price | Gross revenue for one launch |

These are sensitivity cases, not forecasts. Capacity, list freshness, message fit, refund rate, and audience permission matter more than the historical learner count. The first beta only needs 20 to 30 buyers to produce useful evidence.

## 90-day validation plan

### Days 1 to 15: validate the problem and permission to sell

- Map the learning objectives, exercises, and promised outcomes of both existing Training Plan AI courses; document what the separate business will not teach.
- Define the boundary between the core Training Plan and the new business, including which platform, staff, support, certification, and marketing capabilities can be shared.
- Review the Intuit agreement, customer communication permissions, and mark usage.
- Pull internal numbers for active learners, recent buyers, AI-course engagement, team accounts, refund rates, live-class attendance, and one-on-one help usage.
- Interview 12 to 15 customers.
- Survey at least 100 relevant customers if the reachable list allows it.
- Choose one workshop promise and one target role.
- Publish a simple landing page and take paid registrations or refundable deposits.

**Continue gate:** At least 20 credible purchase intents, 10 paid deposits, or enough paid registrations to cover direct delivery cost. Also seek at least three qualified team conversations.

### Days 16 to 45: deliver the first professional beta

- Cap enrollment at 25.
- Use an instructor and TA.
- Require each person to work on one sanitized real-world workflow.
- Measure confidence and task performance before and after.
- Collect time-to-complete, completion, refund, help-request, and follow-on interest.
- Interview at least five participants after the workshop.

**Continue gate:**

- at least 70% complete the intended workflow
- at least 80% say the workshop delivered the stated outcome
- Net Promoter Score of at least 40, used as a directional measure
- refunds below 10%
- positive contribution after direct labor
- at least 20% ask for a next course, team version, or ongoing help

### Days 46 to 90: test repeatability and B2B value

- Run a second public cohort at a higher price.
- Sell and deliver one private team workshop if possible.
- Turn the beta into an instructor guide, timing plan, exercise pack, and update checklist.
- Decide which parts become self-paced prerequisites and which must remain live.
- Build the simplest possible new-brand landing site on top of the existing operating stack.
- Conduct youth legal, privacy, and vendor-access review.
- Run only a small invitation-based youth pilot if the safeguards are ready.

**Scale gate:** At least 15 full-price public seats, one repeatable delivery by an instructor other than the course author or a documented path to that outcome, and one serious team buyer or signed private workshop.

## Main risks and mitigations

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic AI content becomes free | Google, vendors, nonprofits, and creators continually publish beginner material | Charge for live outcomes, judgment, feedback, support, assessment, and role-specific workflows |
| Tool features change monthly | Demonstrations and screenshots can become obsolete quickly | Separate durable concepts from dated, replaceable labs; assign review owners |
| Brand confusion with Intuit endorsement | Customers may assume the broader AI business is endorsed by Intuit | Use a separate URL and marks; review contracts and all trust-transfer language |
| Existing AI courses are cannibalized | A broad beginner workshop could make one or both current products feel redundant | Give the Training Plan ownership of QuickBooks-centered AI learning; require a written overlap audit and make the new offer cross-functional, team-based, and outcome-specific |
| Youth privacy or account rules are mishandled | Minors, recordings, progress data, and vendor age terms create trust and legal exposure | Parent customer model, minimum data, documented consent, vendor review, deletion schedule, trained staff |
| Instructor quality does not scale | A workshop can depend too heavily on one expert | Instructor guide, TA model, recorded calibration, observation rubric, train-the-trainer path |
| Buyers experiment but do not change behavior | Interest in AI does not guarantee adoption | Require a real workflow, manager support, a 30-day plan, and follow-up measurement |
| A large catalog is built before demand is proven | Content creation can consume months without validating willingness to pay | Presell one workshop, then expand only from demonstrated requests |

## What not to do yet

- Do not build a separate LMS before testing sales.
- Do not launch with six unrelated workshops.
- Do not lead with “prompt engineering” as the entire value proposition.
- Do not claim that historical learners equal the current addressable market.
- Do not assume the Intuit endorsement transfers to the new business.
- Do not make minors create accounts that violate vendor age rules.
- Do not distribute recordings containing minors without a purpose-limited consent and access model.
- Do not promise productivity, revenue, career, or admissions outcomes that have not been measured.
- Do not make the youth product carry the full burden of proving the business model.

## Decisions needed from David

1. Define which QuickBooks Training capabilities can be shared without changing the customer experience or focus of the core Training Plan.
2. Assign ownership of the boundary between the two current AI courses and the separate brand, including who approves topics that could fit either business.
3. Decide whether the first commercial goal is new revenue, strategic diversification, a mission-driven youth program, or a deliberate mix.
4. Decide whether the separate brand should publicly identify its relationship to QuickBooksTraining.com and whether existing customers may be invited to its offers.
5. Choose the first professional audience: team-leading bookkeepers, accounting firms, small-business owners, operations managers, or another existing segment.
6. Decide how much custom team work the company wants to deliver. Private training is attractive economically but behaves more like a service business.
7. Decide whether **Be Smarter Than the Tool** is intended to be paid, sponsored, freely accessible, or a mixture.
8. Name an internal owner for fast-changing AI content and vendor policy review.

## Bottom line

The evidence supports a paid test. It does not yet support a major standalone investment.

The company already has most of the expensive machinery that a new training business would need. Its best initial advantage is the overlap between AI adoption, small-business workflows, and an existing audience that trusts the company to teach practical software. The two current AI courses prove that AI education is now a real Training Plan category. They also establish a boundary: the separate business has to solve broader, cross-functional, or organizational problems that QuickBooks-centered courses do not.

The most sensible path is:

> **Use the existing AI learners to validate demand for cross-functional and team training, keep QuickBooks-centered AI education in the Training Plan, build the separate brand only as distinct demand becomes visible, and develop the youth line with a separate safety and privacy standard.**

If one public workshop and one private team offer can be sold, delivered, repeated, and measured within 90 days, the idea has earned a broader business plan. If they cannot, the company will have learned that before funding a catalog, a new platform, or a large customer-acquisition effort.
