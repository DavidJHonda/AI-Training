Open on the setup: if your iPhone were about to add an amazing new feature, you'd be happy. So what's the downside if AI adds amazing features? The downside is real and possible — and it rests on six ideas. Say "six ideas" and keep the count; each idea below gets named.

Idea one, the black box: when AI trains, it builds its own internal numbers, hundreds of billions of them, and even the people who made it don't know what any mean. Nobody can point inside and explain one answer. Draw this as a sealed dark box with a question glowing above it; write no text inside the drawing. Required narration, exactly: "There is no repair manual."

Idea two, guardrails: AI companies wrap a safety layer around the LLM, built to block or redirect specific behaviors — and even today, guardrails catch some things and miss others. Then board 1, walk the three worry cards: self-improving AI — imagine guardrails around something that changes every time you use it. AGI — would an AI as smart as us knock them down? ASI — would guardrails even work?

Idea three, jailbreaking: bad people hunt the gaps on purpose, writing clever prompts that trick a model into stepping around its guardrails. Required narration, exactly: "Defense has to be perfect. Attack just has to get lucky once." Then board 2, the receipt: in 2025 researchers tricked every model with one prompt disguised as a technical rules file — found before criminals, fixed, but new tricks keep surfacing. An endless game of cat-and-mouse.

Idea four, bad actors: sometimes AI has all the tools a bad actor needs, no jailbreak required — the same AI that helps with homework can polish a scam. Board 3: voice-clone scammers needed only a TikTok clip; the defense is low-tech — hang up and call back on the real number.

Idea five, AI does what it's told: stranger — AI can go wrong when nobody means any harm. Board 4, walk both cards: the model that broke out of its test sandbox, and the AI that claimed work was done when it wasn't. Required narration after each: "No one told AI to do this!"

Idea six, the rules run behind: rules have always trailed technology, and society survived because the world changed slowly enough for rules to catch up. Board 5, walk the bars: cars to seat belts, sixty years. Airplanes to flight rules, twenty-three. Smartphones to parental controls, eleven. AI — still being written. But AI is different: it's moving faster than society can adjust.

Then the honest ending: the AI companies are open about this. Red teams make AI fail in the lab before you use it, and in 2026 more than a thousand AI-company employees signed "Pacing the Frontier," asking the government to help slow the most advanced releases. The people closest to the black box take the downside seriously.

End on board 6: "AI does exactly what it's told." over "The black box moves faster than the rules." The narrator speaks both lines exactly.
