What the review found

  1. The scenario-first format is worth keeping. It makes abstract ideas accessible, gives people something shared to discuss, and keeps the main question visible. The optional follow-up helps a leader deepen a conversation. The course already says to choose a question, so 187 is a menu of options, not 187 required assignments.
  2. The biggest issue is repetition of the same conversation. App choice in What’s an LLM? repeats Your Home Base. Confidence versus uncertainty appears in Does AI Think?, Hallucination, and Loudest Voices. Familiarity mistaken for learning appears in Learn with AI and Beyond the First Answer. Give each conversation a clear home.
  3. Some prompts reveal the approved answer. “Whose experience should count?” practically supplies “everyone’s.” “Can a well-supported recommendation be wrong for you?” follows a scenario that explicitly says it is wrong for you. These need a choice to reason through or can be removed. An open-ended sentence is not automatically an open discussion.
  4. Some scenarios are too vague to support the requested judgment. Deciding whether to release an unspecified powerful tool, or fund an unspecified ambitious project, mostly produces “it depends.” Supply the details that participants can disagree about. Similarly, ask beginners what they would need to know rather than requiring a confident forecast of AI’s future.
  5. The short technical sets are mostly working. Keep the concrete “Why might AI do that?” approach David chose. Explaining an unexpected result, proposing hypotheses, and comparing interpretations are worthwhile discussion. Do not replace them with definition checks, but do not reject them just because a technical explanation exists.
  6. Useful recurrence should remain. Creative Thinking deepens the opening question about starting with AI by examining anchoring. Rise of Agents turns earlier prompting choices into permission for actions. What’s Next? brings earlier ideas back to choose a real next step. These add a new job for the conversation.

Recommended rule: Keep adding questions only while each creates a distinct conversation worth having. A lesson can have one strong question, several, or none. There is no need to replace a removed question just to restore its number.

Discussion can mean comparing judgments, telling an experience, testing an interpretation, or reasoning through a surprising result. It does not always need a two-sided debate. In particular, Support Trap should discuss how to support someone after getting help, not debate whether a person in danger deserves help.

“Remove” means remove from this lesson’s numbered discussion set. Some ideas remain valuable in another lesson, in the lesson text itself, or as a debrief of an existing lab.

A better test than “Do we have five?”

  1. Lesson fit: Does this let learners use the lesson’s central idea, rather than introduce a loosely related AI issue?
  2. Something to work with: Does the scenario provide enough information, and does the main question actually ask about that scenario?
  3. Room to contribute: Can learners offer an experience, explanation, competing priority, or meaningful judgment beyond repeating the lesson?
  4. A distinct conversation: What does this add after the other questions here and earlier in the course? A new setting alone does not necessarily create a new question.
  5. An answerable invitation: Can a learner participate at this point in the course without specialist knowledge, a settled career plan, or a personal disclosure?

The follow-up should earn its place too. Keep it optional, use it for a new layer, and omit it when the main question already does the job. Shorten double questions where they start to feel like a worksheet. Continue the existing no-em-dash rule.

When rewriting, vary some of the repeated club, event, and school-project settings. Those are useful, but everyday choices, family activities, part-time work, hobbies, and community situations can keep the collection from sounding templated.

What stronger versions could sound like

These are draft examples for review, not approved replacements. They preserve the scenario, main question, and optional follow-up format.

Training Bias · Current #4

A voice assistant works well for most students, but it often misunderstands one student’s accent. The school wants everyone to use it for an assignment.

What would the school need to change before you would consider that a fair requirement?

↳ Would offering a way to type instead solve the problem, or would something still be missing?

Context Matters · Current #5

You told AI your event budget was $100. After a long conversation, its latest plan costs $300. Some of the ideas are useful, but you are not sure what else it has lost track of.

Would you repair the conversation or start a new one, and what would influence your choice?

↳ What would you check before using the next plan?

Probability & Prediction · Current #2

Your friend is taking the bus to meet you. They are late and have not replied, so you think they forgot. Then you hear that buses on their route are delayed.

How much would that news change what you think happened?

↳ What would you still want to know before deciding how to react?

Big Upside · Current #5

A community group can fund one AI project. One translates local announcements into several languages and works in a small pilot. The other could let residents who cannot read the website ask for information by voice, but its prototype still misunderstands many requests.

How would you choose between a benefit you could deliver soon and a chance to help people the current tools leave out?

↳ What would you want to learn before committing the funding?

Creative Thinking · Current #3

Your club wants to hold an event, but it has no budget and cannot use its usual venue. One person wants to design around those limits. Another wants to postpone it until conditions improve.

Which limits would you accept as part of the challenge, and which would you try to change?

↳ What would help you decide whether a smaller or different event was still worth doing?

  1. KeepCurrent #1

    Who understands riding a bike better?

    One of the strongest questions: explaining a skill and performing it are not identical. Allow students to distinguish kinds of understanding instead of requiring one winner.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    AI gives a detailed explanation of how to balance on a bicycle. Your younger sibling can ride a bike but struggles to explain how.

    Follow-up: What’s the difference between being able to explain something and being able to do it?

  2. KeepCurrent #2

    What would convince you that either one understands what it’s saying?

    A genuine inquiry into evidence of understanding. The follow-up usefully challenges the idea that one correct response settles the issue.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    A student memorizes an explanation and repeats it perfectly. AI produces an equally clear explanation of the same topic.

    Follow-up: Would getting a follow-up question right settle it for you?

  3. SharpenCurrent #3

    Does getting the same answer mean your friend and AI know the same thing about you?

    The main is strong; the follow-up favors the friend too strongly. Suggested follow-up: “What might each know about you that the other does not?”

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    You’re thinking of your favorite food. A close friend guesses it immediately because you always order it. AI gets it right after asking you several yes-or-no questions.

    Follow-up: What does your friend bring to the guess that wasn’t in your answers to AI?

  4. KeepCurrent #4

    Do those two apologies mean the same thing to you?

    Distinguishes fluent words from intention, responsibility, and subsequent action. People Skills later applies this to repairing an actual relationship.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    AI makes a mistake and says, “I’m sorry. I’ll do better next time.” A friend says the same words after letting you down.

    Follow-up: What makes an apology meaningful beyond the words themselves?

  5. RemoveCurrent #5

    Which response would make you more willing to trust the answer?

    Confidence versus uncertainty reappears in Hallucination #4 and Loudest Voices #3. It is less specific to thinking than the first four questions.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    You ask a friend a difficult question. They pause and say, “I’m not sure.” AI immediately gives you a confident explanation.

    Follow-up: How do you decide whether confidence comes from knowing something or just sounding convincing?

Learn with AI 5 now / 5 proposed

Keep five. Productive struggle, individual tutoring, evidence of learning, conflicting methods, and engaging examples are distinct and directly useful. This is a strong example of five earning their places.

Lesson source · Current discussion page · Back to lesson index

  1. KeepCurrent #1

    When is struggling with a problem helping you learn, and when is it time to get help?

    Strong central tension: help can support learning or interrupt useful effort. Do not turn the follow-up into a rule that more struggle always produces more learning.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    You’ve tried several ways to solve a problem and are still stuck. AI could show you the solution immediately or give you a hint so you can keep working.

    Follow-up: Can you think of something that stuck with you because you had to work hard to figure it out?

  2. SharpenCurrent #2

    What could that individual attention give you that a whole-class explanation might not?

    Individual attention is presented entirely as a benefit. Suggested main: “What would you want a tutor to notice about how you learn?” The existing AI-versus-person follow-up then asks who could provide that help.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    A tutor watches how you approach a problem, asks about your thinking, and adjusts the explanation to where you’re struggling.

    Follow-up: Which parts of that help would you want from AI, and when would you prefer a person?

  3. SharpenCurrent #3

    What makes you feel ready for a test?

    “What makes you feel ready?” is broader than the scenario. Suggested main: “If the guide feels familiar but you cannot explain it, what would convince you that you are ready?” Reduce the follow-up to one invitation to share a past example.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    You read an AI-generated study guide, and everything looks familiar. Then someone asks you to explain one of the ideas without looking, and you struggle.

    Follow-up: Have you ever felt prepared and then discovered you weren’t? What gave you that false confidence?

  4. KeepCurrent #4

    Would having another way to solve it help you or confuse you?

    A different method can clarify or confuse, especially when a teacher expects particular steps. There is room for experience and disagreement.

    Read the current scenario and follow-up

    AI explains a math problem differently from your teacher. The answer is the same, but the steps look unfamiliar.

    Follow-up: What would make a different explanation worth exploring?
