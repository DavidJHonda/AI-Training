# 8. Work Changes

**[0:00]** Before you know it, you'll be finishing school and starting your career. Familiar job titles like entrepreneur, teacher, lawyer, or designer will still exist, but the daily reality of those roles is already changing.

**[0:13]** If everyone has access to these tools and gets similarly capable answers from the AI, what sets you apart from anyone else? The answer is what you already know. Let's look at how this plays out in a real, entry-level scenario. This image lays out your first assignment. Your manager tells you we're getting bad customer feedback on our new product. Read the last 500 reviews and tell us what's happening and how we should fix it. In the old job, before AI, the entire week disappears into the first pass. Now let's look at the new job, with AI. The AI completes that entire first pass in minutes. The AI reads the reviews, finds the themes, builds the spreadsheet and drafts the deck. work. You start your work by improving what the AI produced.

**[0:59]** You spend your time investigating the why, recommending a fix, and presenting findings. Because the mechanical labor is out of the way, your work week begins immediately with the real questions. But improving and contextualizing that work requires what you already know. As you navigate these new workflows, you'll hear two specific terms used to categorize how these tools handle professional tasks. This graphic shows the distinction clearly. On the left, we have automate. This is when the AI fully takes over a mechanical step, like sorting the reviews, grouping the ideas, and creating that first baseline summary. On the right is augment. Here, the AI acts as a tool to expand what you can do. It helps you explore more explanations, compare more options, and ultimately improve your final recommendation.

**[1:48]** But beneath both of these methods, lies a non-negotiable rule of modern work. The work still has your name on it. You own the outcome.

**[1:56]** Historically, grunt work, like basic research and starter code or simple data analysis, was the primary way young professionals actually learned their trade. Now, the AI handles a lot of that initial labor. Because of this, you face a strange paradox. AI can make you productive before it makes you knowledgeable. This creates a subtle trap. In your early career, you will likely be asked to verify and approve highly sophisticated outputs from the AI, long before you have built the underlying expertise required to spot its errors. Your only defense against this trap is relentless learning. You have to build that expertise yourself, making your time in school right now the greatest advantage you could possibly have. AI is changing the work you'll do. Now it's on you to learn.
