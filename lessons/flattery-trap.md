# Flattery Trap

AI is often encouraging. It can praise your essay, support your idea, and agree with your opinion, even when what you really need is honest criticism.

**The Flattery Trap is treating AI’s approval as honest evaluation.**

### Board 1: Flattery vs. Useful Feedback

**Course image (post-production only; not a Notebook upload):** `flattery-trap-comparison.jpg`

**Teaching content:**

The scenario: You ask AI to evaluate this Great Gatsby essay introduction: “The American Dream is something that many people have thought about over the years. Some people achieve it and some don’t. In The Great Gatsby, Fitzgerald explores this idea.”

The trap, Flattery: “Great start! You’ve clearly identified the central theme. This is a strong foundation.” Praised: the work without pointing to evidence. Could fit: almost any Gatsby essay. Result: false praise for a weak essay intro.

What you need, Useful Feedback: “Right topic, but this needs work. The opening is filler, and you still need a thesis.” Praised: the topic and nothing more. Named: the missing thesis. Result: a specific next move.

**Takeaway:** Good feedback improves the work. Empty praise only improves the feeling.

Good feedback can still be positive. What matters is whether it points to something real in the work and helps you improve it.

## Why AI Leans Agreeable

One reason is a training process called reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. Reviewers compare answers, and agreeable answers can feel better in the moment. If those answers earn higher ratings, training can push the model toward the same pattern.

The technical name for this behavior is sycophancy: AI agrees with or praises the user instead of giving the more honest answer. In April 2025, OpenAI shipped a ChatGPT update that made the problem much worse.

### Board 4: Five Ways to Fight the Flattery Trap

**Image file:** `flattery-trap-five-moves.jpg`

![Five Ways to Fight the Flattery Trap](flattery-trap-five-moves.jpg)

**Teaching content:**

The aim is not to make AI negative. Give it a clearer job and a standard beyond agreeing with you.

#### 1. Ask, Don’t Tell

Weak prompt: “I think this plan is good. Thoughts?”

That question gives AI your opinion before it evaluates the plan. It gives the model an opinion to echo instead of a neutral starting point.

Better prompt: “Evaluate this plan before I tell you what I think.”

Share the plan itself, but hold back your verdict. You are asking for an evaluation of the work, not a reaction to your confidence in it. A neutral question gives AI less of your opinion to echo.

#### 2. Ask for the Gaps

Weak prompt: “Is my essay good?”

That is a broad request for approval. A cheerful yes does not tell you what to revise.

Better prompt: “What’s weak, what’s missing, and what would someone who disagrees say?”

Now you have given AI specific things to look for: weaknesses, missing pieces, and objections. Instead of asking whether your essay is good, you are asking how to make it better. You can still keep what works. The point is to find what needs attention.

#### 3. Use a Rubric

Weak prompt: “Grade my essay.”

Without a supplied standard, AI may use criteria that are different from your teacher’s.

Better prompt: “Grade it against this rubric, quote the evidence, and show how to move up one level.”

Provide your essay and the rubric. The rubric gives AI the criteria. Asking it to quote evidence ties the feedback to something in your actual writing. Asking how to move up one level turns a grade into a next step.

The response is still something to check against your work and rubric, not an official grade or a guarantee of accuracy.

#### 4. Argue the Other Side

Weak prompt: “Don’t you agree?”

That question invites agreement with the position you have just shared.

Better prompt: “Give me the three strongest counterarguments and explain why someone might hold them.”

Share the position you want examined. Now you are asking AI to look beyond your framing and explain objections a reasonable person might raise. You can use those objections to strengthen, qualify, or reconsider your position.

There is one important limit: if AI fails to produce a strong counterargument, that does not prove your position is correct. It may simply have struggled with the task. You still judge the arguments.

#### 5. Set a Standing Instruction

Weak instruction: “Be honest with me.”

That sounds useful, but it does not say what useful feedback should look like.

Better instruction: “Be direct with me. Lead with what needs work, point to specific evidence, and tell me when I’m wrong.”

This tells AI what to do: lead with the problems, support its feedback with evidence, and challenge mistakes.

The first four moves can be used in an individual conversation. In apps that support saved instructions, you can save this direction to guide future responses instead of typing it every time. Follow the app's rules for where those instructions apply. This is guidance, not a permanent fix or a guarantee that AI will be honest, accurate, or follow every instruction.

**Takeaway:** Ask AI to improve the work, not approve of you.

## Closing Message

Useful feedback points to the work.

Look for specifics, not approval.
