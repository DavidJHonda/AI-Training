Focus: what you do with an AI answer, knowing it might be wrong.

Open on the problem the lesson answers: AI makes mistakes, and it uses the same confident voice whether the facts are right or wrong. So what do you do with an answer that might be wrong? One option is to ignore AI — and nobody this far into the course is doing that, because AI is too powerful to leave on the shelf. Name the course: to Be Smarter Than the Tool, you need the skills to evaluate the results.

Then the five-steps board, held while the narration walks all five rows in order, speaking the reason written under each, in the board's own words. Do not read the five names and move on. Three lines must land as written: reading every word is the floor; the fastest explainer of an AI answer is often the same AI; a well-stocked brain is your fastest fact-checker, and this is where everything you've ever learned pays off.

Then the Petronoski example, given real time; it is the sharpest thing in the lesson. "Maria Petronoski won 3 gold medals at the 1932 Olympics in Athens" sounds perfectly fine. Say both facts aloud: the 1932 Games were in Los Angeles, and she doesn't exist. Then the rule — the more specific the detail, the more skeptical you should be — and the limit: when a topic is beyond what you know, "sounds fine" isn't validation.

Step four, decide, is two questions. What kind of task was this: fact work needs more evaluation, while a first draft or brainstorm just gets checked against what you asked for. And how much is riding on it: a movie for tonight, you're done; a college essay, a health question, anything with your name on it, keep going.

Then the dig-deeper board, held while the narration walks its rows, speaking the reason written under each. Two must land in full: if you can't find the source, assume it doesn't exist, because AI frequently invents plausible-sounding citations; and an answer can be all true and still narrow, which is why you ask who's missing. End on leaving the chat — search the claim yourself, and take health, legal or crisis questions to a real person.

Then the relief, so the lesson doesn't land as a chore: read, understand, validate takes seconds, and for most chats you won't need to dig deeper. You'll just chat away.

Then the three-outcomes board, held while the narration walks all three — use it, fix it, walk away — speaking the reason written under each, in the board's own words.

Close board: "Know which answers deserve the dig." over "Read. Understand. Validate."
