Focus: the answer can be entirely true and still be distorted, because the model learned whatever was in the data it saw.

Open where the lesson opens, on the unsettling part: sometimes AI invents nothing at all. Every fact in the answer is real, and the picture is still distorted. Then the cause: the model learns whatever patterns are in the data it saw, so if that data is uneven, incomplete, or full of shortcuts, it can treat a narrow slice of reality as the default.

Then the lesson's driving example. If the only driving advice online came from professional race car drivers, you ask "what's the best way to drive?" and the model might tell you to drive 180 miles per hour on a closed track. Say the point aloud: not evil intent, just a narrow slice of data treated like the whole picture.

Then cows on the beach, as a story with a turn in it. Researchers had a model that spotted cows with high accuracy. Then they showed it a cow on a beach and it went blank — same animal, same shape, same spots. The training photos almost all showed cows on green pasture, so it learned the wrong pattern: green grass means cow. Land the two short lines exactly as written: it never learned the animal, it learned the background.

Then the mechanisms board. Introduce it as the lesson does: not one mechanism but four overlapping ones, all rooted in the training data. Hold the board and walk all four rows in order — overrepresentation, underrepresentation, shortcuts, biased answer key — speaking the reason written under each one, in the board's own words. Never read the four names and move on.

Then the hardest idea, and give it room: there is no fact-check for this trap, because every fact in a distorted answer can check out. Fact-checks cover what's on the page; bias hides in what never made the page. That is why the Evaluate the Results dig list asks who's missing — name that lesson.

Then the stale-information board, introduced as the lesson does: one more data trap, and this one isn't bias or hallucination. Hold it and walk its three cards in order — what happens, why it isn't a hallucination, the fix — speaking the reason written under each, in the board's own words. The middle card carries the whole idea: it isn't inventing, it's remembering, and the memory is out of date.

Close board: "The model learned our shortcuts." over "Including the ones we'd rather it didn't."
