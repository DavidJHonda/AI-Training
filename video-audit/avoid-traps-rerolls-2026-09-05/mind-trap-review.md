# Mind Trap — evaluation

Source: `Prompts/mind-trap.mp4` · 4:19 · September 5, 2026

**Recommendation: repair, with a heavier editorial pass.** The essential lesson survives: fluent language can feel like personal understanding, and important decisions still need your judgment and people who know you. The main weakness is abstract language replacing a more concrete, student-friendly example.

## What works

**0:34–0:45** makes fluency versus familiarity clear. **1:43–2:03** explains ELIZA’s patterned questions and the feeling of being understood. **2:16–2:49** contains the two-part mechanism needed for our current board. **3:18–3:26** keeps useful roles for AI, while **3:36–3:47** restores human knowledge and shared consequences. The closing “Use AI to think. You make the call” is right.

## Suggested changes

1. **0:13–0:18 versus 0:26–0:34 — Resolve the opening contradiction.** First the student gives AI anxieties, finances, and career goals; then the video says its response has no genuine context. My first choice is to cut the added “dump all your…” sentence and use the current board’s simple Michigan/Indiana question. Then the generic-answer contrast makes sense.
2. **0:45–1:08 — Use the current college comparison board to ground the mother’s advice.** The narration discusses silence, body language, history, and shared burden, but never gives her actual recommendation or the useful reason: the student needs an environment where they will ask for help. Do not pretend the narration reads that quote. Show the complete mother’s column long enough to read it, then use the relevant subtopics as spoken. If the existing wording competes with the quote, simplify this span rather than adding a dense, unreadable overlay.
3. **2:03–2:16 — Cut the “psychologists officially named this cognitive glitch” / “proved…effortlessly override” claim.** The ELIZA story is enough; this adds unsupported authority and makes a tendency sound inevitable. The next sentence still names the ELIZA effect. The [original ELIZA paper](https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/331/papers/eliza.html) supports the scripted conversational mechanism, not this sweeping proof claim.
4. **3:00–3:07 — Cut “not successfully mimicking empathy” and the ghost-in-the-machine line.** It contradicts the earlier explanation that software generates empathetic-sounding phrases. Simulating the language is precisely what helps create the effect; that is different from having human feelings or a relationship.
5. **3:26–3:29 — Cut “that is where its utility ends.”** The preceding examples are useful examples, not an exhaustive boundary on AI’s usefulness.
6. **3:48–4:12 — Tighten the abstract ending.** “Data collection can be synthetic,” “consequence-bearing,” and “a permanent shift in your reality” are less clear than the course’s closing message. Keep the people-who-know-you point, then transition to the current close. “Synthetic” also risks suggesting fabricated data is acceptable.
