## Background: Uber and Lyft

- [Consumer Reports investigation](https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/uber-lyft-different-prices-for-same-ride-and-fake-discounts-a1093538909/) and [method/data repository](https://github.com/consumerreports/lyft-uber). Published in June 2026 from testing conducted in March and April 2026. Reports different quotes for comparable rides requested close together and identifies some advertised discounts as misleading because they appeared to use inflated reference prices. These tests do not prove that personal data set a particular rider’s base fare.
- [Lyft pricing response](https://www.lyft.com/blog/posts/lets-talk-about-lyft-pricing). Says base pricing responds to current supply, demand, traffic, time, and location, and denies using purchase history, personal spending habits, or off-platform behavior for base fares. Lyft’s [May 2026 quarterly filing](https://investor.lyft.com/financials/sec-filings/content/0001628280-26-032412/lyft-20260331.htm) says the company uses AI and machine-learning technologies and algorithms to optimize pricing and its marketplace.
- [Uber 2026 algorithmic transparency report](https://tb-static.uber.com/prod/uber-static/uber-sites/_pdf/ai-on-uber/US-Algorithmic-Transparency-2026.pdf). Company disclosure supporting the lesson’s limited statement that AI and machine learning are used in marketplace pricing. The lesson does not claim CR proved individualized willingness-to-pay pricing.
- [Journal of Law and Economics, “Does Uber Benefit Travelers by Price Discrimination?”](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/721266). Background only, not part of the student-facing story or active video narration. Uses 2018 UberX airport-to-hotel quotes. Reports a positive association between hotel room prices and fares after controls, interprets that as third-degree price discrimination, and estimates average traveler benefits from expanded transportation options.

## Background: Rite Aid

- [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/12/rite-aid-banned-using-ai-facial-recognition-after-ftc-says-retailer-deployed-technology-without) and [case page](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/cases-proceedings/2023190-rite-aid-corporation-ftc-v). FTC allegations concern use from 2012 to 2020, false matches, inadequate safeguards, and employee actions. The court order imposes a five-year facial-recognition prohibition for security or surveillance.
- [FTC complaint](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2023190_riteaid_complaint_filed.pdf). Paragraphs describe the 11-year-old girl being stopped and searched after a false-positive match.
- [Associated Press report](https://apnews.com/article/e8deb16296d9cef78faca2761b2efb48). Includes Rite Aid’s response that it disagreed with the allegations, described the system as a limited pilot, and had stopped using it more than three years earlier.

## DraftKings

- [New York Times, “How DraftKings Uses A.I. to Target the Gamblers Likeliest to Lose”](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/19/business/draftkings-ai.html). Investigation based on internal documents and interviews with more than 40 former employees. Reports that promotional models predicted additional gambling losses following incentives, using betting frequency, balances, typical losses, and other behavioral information. Promotional work began with casino customers and expanded to sports betting. Employees raised concerns about vulnerable gamblers. Separate predictive problem-gambling work was developed but not deployed. DraftKings disputed loss-based targeting; its responsible gaming chief acknowledged declining predictive risk technology, citing insufficient evidence and a preference for existing safeguards. The lesson does not claim the rejected models were proven effective or that DraftKings lacks protective measures.

It asks what protections a company should require, even if they reduce revenue; revenue and protection are not presented as mutually exclusive.
